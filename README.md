## The four findings that change the thesis

### 1. Almost nowhere on earth can a private party own an alpine summit

Above the treeline is Crown land (New Zealand, Canada), 国有林 national forest inside 国立公園
national park (Japan), a US Forest Service permit (United States), *monte público* (Spain),
*statsallmenning* (Norway), or reserved to citizens (Georgia, Kyrgyzstan, Mongolia, Lesotho).
**Greenland has no private land ownership at all.**

**Two exceptions exist, and both are historical accidents:** the **1872 General Mining Law**,
under which a *patented* US mining claim conveyed outright fee — which is why several Colorado
and Montana summits are privately held; and Scotland's post-feudal estates, whose boundaries run
over Munro summits.

**This is why Gate 1 is tenure, not price.** A buyer who screens on snow or cost first will
spend months on assets that can never satisfy the mandate.

### 2. In New Zealand, the snow line and the freehold line were deliberately separated

The snow screen wants a base at 1,500–1,600 m. Tenure review (1998–2022) **freeholded the low
country and transferred the alpine tops to the Crown** — Godley Peaks freeholded 2,676 ha while
**11,875 ha went to conservation** — and the Crown Pastoral Land Reform Act 2022 closed the
process, so there is no conversion path left.

> **New Zealand freehold is, by construction, *below* the snow line.** The highest freehold ski
> terrain found in the country is Mt Lyford at ~1,460 m.

This is not a market accident. It is the direct result of 24 years of deliberate policy
separating exactly the two things this mandate needs to combine — and it is only visible by
reading the tenure and snow evidence together.

### 3. A cheap alpine station is not a low-cost one

Carrying cost is **almost entirely independent of purchase price.** Rates follow a low rural
valuation; pest control, tracks, staff, insurance and compliance follow **area, condition and
remoteness**.

| Purchase price | Total annual cost of ownership @ 5 % | Net carry as % of price |
|---|---|---:|
| NZ$3 m | NZ$776k (**RM 1.87 m**) | **20.9 %** ⚠️ |
| NZ$8 m | NZ$1,026k (RM 2.48 m) | 7.8 % |
| NZ$35 m | NZ$2,376k (RM 5.74 m) | 1.8 % |

**At NZ$3 m you re-spend the purchase price in carry roughly every five years.** What actually
determines "low cost to hold", in order: **pest condition > existing access and buildings >
ETS-eligible area > assignable grazing lease > total area > purchase price (least important).**

And the ranch does not pay the rates: pastoral EBIT on a modelled 8,000 ha station is
**NZ$0–150k/yr against a NZ$346k–1,210k carry.** Farming is a cost-neutraliser, not a profit
centre.

### 4. The regulatory step-change is `no lift → any lift`, not `surface lift → chairlift`

Rope tows, T-bars and platters are **regulated passenger ropeways in both New Zealand** (PECPR
Regulations 1999, NZS 8635:2018) **and Japan** (鉄道事業法 特殊索道, requiring MLIT permission
per installation, a 索道技術管理者 and annual inspection). In the **United States** the
equivalent line is the **paying-customer** line, not the lift line — private non-charging use
keeps recreational-use statute protection, which is lost the moment you charge.

And a relocated second-hand T-bar is a trap: purchase is 10–20 % of delivered cost, so a
US$60k lift becomes **US$0.8–2.6 m installed — 70–100 % of new.**

> **Phase 1 should be snowcat, snowmobile and touring, indefinitely.** That is not a compromise;
> in three jurisdictions independently it is the design that avoids an entire regulatory regime
> and the largest recurring cost line.

## Three things to settle before spending money

1. **Bank Negara Malaysia outward-investment capacity.** A resident individual **with domestic
   ringgit borrowing** is reportedly capped at **RM 1 million per calendar year** of
   foreign-currency investment; without such borrowing, no limit. **Every candidate here costs
   far more.** Confirm in writing with the client's own bank — this is a structural constraint
   on the whole mandate, not a compliance detail.
2. **Is a defunct ski area "farm land" for Overseas Investment Act purposes?** If **not**, it
   escapes the 2025 farmland carve-out and the low-cost operating model becomes consentable in
   New Zealand. Worth NZ$100–250k/yr and the difference between running one business and four.
3. **Liability insurance quotations** for the intended operating mode in each shortlisted
   jurisdiction. New Zealand's ACC bars compensatory personal-injury claims entirely; the US
   equivalent runs US$60–350k/yr. **An uninsurable operation is not a business** — this is a
   go/no-go input.

## Evidence standard, and the limits of this pass

Every material claim is graded `[P1]` primary · `[P2]` reputable secondary · `[P3]` broker
marketing claim (**never treated as fact**) · `[E]` estimate with derivation shown · `[U]`
unverified. See [`00-methodology/02-evidence-standard.md`](00-methodology/02-evidence-standard.md).

**Two constraints materially limited this pass, and both are disclosed rather than papered
over:**

- The research environment's **network policy blocked direct page retrieval** from Bayleys,
  NZ Sotheby's, LINZ, RNZ, Wikipedia, BATONZ, スキー凸凹研究所, JMA and every government domain
  attempted. Facts from those sources are carried at the grade of the secondary source that
  reported them.
- The **web-search budget was exhausted** partway through. Seven of nine workstreams ran out
  mid-task. **No Chilean or US property listings were obtained at all** — the two jurisdictions
  the evidence points at hardest — and the economics and FX figures are model structure with
  scaffolding numbers, not quotations.

**No title searches were ordered, and no site was visited.** `05-verification/` specifies
exactly what to commission before an offer.
