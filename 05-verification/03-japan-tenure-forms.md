# If you cannot get freehold in Japan, a lease is the *weakest* option — not the only one

The Itoigawa work established that Japanese municipalities dispose of ski areas by **lease plus a
free loan of plant**, keeping title. The natural conclusion — *"so I can only be an operator"* — is
right about municipalities but **wrong about Japanese law**, which offers a materially stronger
instrument.

---

## The three forms, ranked

| | **所有権 Ownership** | **地上権 Superficies** | **賃借権 Lease** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legal nature | Full title | **物権 — a real right** | **債権 — a contract right** |
| Registrable in the 登記? | Yes | **Yes, and enforceable against third parties** | Only if the landlord agrees to register — and he usually will not |
| Survives a sale of the land? | n/a | **Yes** | **Not reliably** without registration |
| **Transfer or sublet** | Free | **Free — no landowner consent required by law** | **Requires the landowner's consent** |
| Can be mortgaged | Yes | **Yes** | No |
| Statutory rent obligation | n/a | **None** — periodic rent is not legally required | **Yes** |
| Minimum term | n/a | **30 years, and the parties may set the term freely above that** | — |
| Maximum term | n/a | **No statutory maximum** | **50 years** (民法 604, as amended, in force 2020) |

`[P2 — Japanese real-estate and legal commentary]`

> ### The point in one line
>
> **地上権 is a property right you can register, sell, mortgage and hold for a century without
> asking the landowner's permission. 賃借権 is a contract that dies with the landlord's goodwill
> and expires at fifty years.**
>
> **If freehold is unavailable, the thing to negotiate for is a registered 地上権 — not a lease.**

---

## Why this matters specifically for a ski slope

There is a technical wrinkle that works **in the client's favour and against it** at the same time,
and it needs Japanese counsel to settle. `[E]`

The **借地借家法 (Land Lease and Building Lease Act)** — which grants strong renewal rights —
applies where the purpose is **建物所有目的**, holding land for the purpose of owning a building on
it.

- **A ski slope with no buildings is probably not 建物所有目的.** So it likely falls outside the
  借地借家法 and back onto the plain Civil Code.
- Under the plain Civil Code, **a 賃貸借 is capped at 50 years (民法 604)** and carries none of the
  Act's renewal protection.
- **But 地上権 has no such cap.** Article 268 leaves the term to agreement, and where no term is set
  the court may fix one — with the customary framing being a very long or indefinite duration.

> **So for a lift-free private ski mountain — exactly what the client intends — the gap between a
> lease and a superficies is at its widest.** A lease would be a weak, consent-dependent,
> 50-year-capped contract. A superficies would be a registered, freely transferable, indefinitely
> renewable property right.

---

## The negotiating implication

When a Japanese municipality or a private landowner says *"we will not sell the land,"* that is
**not** the end of the conversation. It is the start of a different one:

1. **Ask for 地上権, registered, for the longest term they will grant.** It costs the landowner
   nothing they were not already conceding, and it is far more valuable to the holder.
2. **Ask for it to be registered** (地上権設定登記). An unregistered right is worth much less.
3. **Confirm free transferability is not contractually excluded** — the default is free, but the
   parties can restrict it by agreement, and a municipality will try.
4. **Consider a 買戻し or 先買権 (right of first refusal)** on the freehold, so that if the
   municipality's position ever changes, the client is first in line.

⚠️ **Caveats requiring Japanese counsel** `[U]`:

- Whether a 公有地 (publicly-owned land) can carry a 地上権 at all under the Local Autonomy Act's
  restrictions on administrative property — **Art. 238-4(1) bars attaching private rights to
  administrative property**, so this only becomes possible **after 用途廃止**, which is the same
  council-vote gate that blocks a sale.
- Whether 借地借家法 applies to a slope with a base lodge (it probably does *for the lodge parcel*),
  which would create a split regime across one property.
- The tax treatment: a 地上権 is a taxable asset in its own right, and its acquisition attracts
  registration tax.

---

## Where this leaves the Japanese options

| Vendor | Realistic instrument | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **A municipality** | Lease + free loan of plant, after multiple council votes | **Weakest.** And the 行政財産 bar means even a 地上権 needs 用途廃止 first |
| **A private company** (the Grandeco pattern) | **所有権 — full freehold, no council vote, no covenants** | **Strongest** |
| A private landowner unwilling to sell | **Registered 地上権, long term** | **Strong — and materially better than the lease the client feared** |

> **The conclusion stands: change the vendor. But if the best terrain is only available from
> someone who will not sell, the fallback is a registered superficies, not a tenancy — and that
> distinction is worth insisting on.**
