## 1. The one filter that decides this brief

The client's requirement is not "a mountain property." It is **legal title that itself contains skiable alpine terrain and peaks.**

`[INFERENCE]` In virtually every mountain country on earth, the zone above treeline is **not** privately ownable. It is Crown land (Canada), state forest and *mera* common pasture (Türkiye), national park (Bulgaria, North Macedonia, Montenegro), *monte de utilidad pública* (Spain), *statsallmenning* (Norway), community pasture committees (Kyrgyzstan), collective land (Morocco), or simply constitutionally reserved to citizens (Georgia, Kazakhstan, Armenia, Mongolia, Lesotho, Greenland).

**The United States is the principal global exception, and Scotland is the secondary one.** `[INFERENCE]` The US has large fee-simple holdings that include summits because of three historical accidents: the Homestead Acts, the 19th-century railroad land grants (which produced the checkerboard of odd-numbered sections across the mountain West), and the **1872 General Mining Law**, under which a *patented* mining claim conveyed outright fee title — which is how a meaningful number of Colorado and Montana summits are in private hands today. Scotland's equivalent accident is that post-feudal abolition (2000/2004) left intact very large, unencumbered private sporting estates whose boundaries run over the tops of Munros.

**Everything else in this brief fails at this filter, or fails on snow, or fails on assembling contiguous scale.** I have killed those quickly below and spent the effort on the three that survive.

## 3. UNITED STATES — RANK 1

### 3.1 Can a Malaysian buy? Yes, and this is the strongest verified finding in the screen.

**Federal:** `[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]` There is no federal prohibition. AFIDA (Agricultural Foreign Investment Disclosure Act 1978) imposes **reporting only** — FSA Form 153 within 90 days of acquisition of agricultural land, with a civil penalty of up to 25% of fair market value for failure to file. This is a compliance chore, not a barrier. CFIUS real-estate jurisdiction (FIRRMA) bites only near listed military installations and airports/ports — relevant to *site selection*, not to nationality.

**State — the 2023–26 wave.** `[SECONDARY]` Verified this session:
- Approximately **36 states** had enacted restrictions on foreign ownership of real property by end-2025, focused on agricultural land, natural resources, critical infrastructure, and proximity to military bases.
- The restrictions **target designated "foreign adversary" / "country of concern" lists**. The foreign-adversary list as of 1 Jan 2025: **China, Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Russia, Syria, Venezuela.**
- 2025 enactments: amendments in Arkansas (HB 1358/HB 1680), Georgia (HB 358), **Idaho (H356/S1149)**, Nebraska (LB 7), Tennessee (HB 219), **Utah (HB 430)**; new laws in Arizona (SB 1082), Kentucky (HB 315), **Texas (SB 17)**, West Virginia (HB 2961).
- 2026 enactments: amendments in Indiana (SB 256), Louisiana, Mississippi, Oklahoma, South Dakota, Tennessee, **Utah (HB 291)**. 25 states considered legislation during 2026.
- **Texas SB 17** (signed 20 Jun 2025, effective 1 Sep 2025) is the template and the warning: it bars citizens of and entities owned from "designated countries" — **currently China, Iran, North Korea, Russia** — from owning *any* real property (agricultural, commercial, industrial, residential, plus water, groundwater and mineral rights) or taking leases of ≥1 year. Designation follows the **Director of National Intelligence Annual Threat Assessment** (a country named in any of the last three), **and the Governor may add countries.**

**`[INFERENCE]` Malaysia is on none of these lists and is not plausibly a near-term addition** — it is a non-aligned ASEAN state with no adversary designation in any US intelligence or sanctions framework. A Malaysian private buyer is therefore, today, in the same position as a US buyer in the mountain West.

**`[INFERENCE]` The residual risk is real but manageable, and it has a specific shape:** the Texas mechanism delegates the list to the DNI assessment *and to the governor*. That is a political variable the client does not control over a 20-year hold. Two mitigations: (i) prefer states whose statute names countries in the *statute itself* rather than delegating to a floating list; (ii) note that most of these statutes are **prospective** and grandfather existing holdings, though some impose divestiture — this must be checked state-by-state before signing.

**`[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]` Separate and important:** a small group of legacy states restrict **all** alien ownership of farmland regardless of nationality — Iowa, Minnesota, North Dakota, Oklahoma, and (with acreage caps) Missouri and Wisconsin. **None of these are mountain states.** The relevant Rocky Mountain / Cascade / Sierra states — Montana, Wyoming, Colorado, Idaho, Utah, Washington, Oregon, California, Nevada, New Mexico — historically have no blanket alien-ownership bar, and the 2025 Idaho and Utah statutes are adversary-targeted, not blanket. **Verify each before offer.**

### 3.2 Deeded vs USFS permit — the single most important diligence point

`[INFERENCE]` The great majority of US lift-served ski terrain sits on **US Forest Service special-use permits**, not on deeded land. The base area is fee; the mountain is permit. This has three consequences the client must internalise:

1. A headline like "*former ski area, 65 acres, US$5.8m*" is almost certainly **base-area fee land plus a permit**, not a mountain. 65 acres cannot contain a ski mountain.
2. USFS special-use permits are **not freely transferable** — they require Forest Service consent and are subject to reissue at the agency's discretion, with current environmental and financial-capability review. A buyer can acquire the base and fail to get the permit.
3. A permit is the opposite of the client's thesis. The thesis is *own the title, run it privately, keep OPEX near zero.* A permit brings annual fees (a percentage-of-revenue formula), agency oversight, NEPA process for any change, and no ownership of the terrain.

**Diligence instruction:** for every US ex-ski-area candidate, pull (a) the recorded deed and a current title commitment with a plotted legal description, (b) the USFS special-use permit file and its status, (c) county GIS overlaying fee parcels on the ski terrain. Do not rely on the acreage in the listing.

### 3.3 The listed closed-ski-area candidates — structural triage

`[INFERENCE]` **I could not verify any of the prices in the brief.** Structurally, though, most of that list is off-thesis:

- **Eastern (Mount Storm NY 88 ac; Mt Jefferson ME 152 ac; Saddleback ME; Hatley Pointe NC; Elk Mountain):** no alpine terrain, low vertical, marginal and warming snow, and acreages far too small for ranch/forestry offset. **Off-thesis.**
- **Western on USFS permit (Iron Mountain CA — Eldorado NF; Cuchara CO — San Isabel NF upper mountain; Antelope Butte WY — Bighorn NF; Sleeping Giant WY — Shoshone NF; Cooper CO — permit, county-linked operator):** you would be buying a base and a permit. Several of these (Antelope Butte, Sleeping Giant) are **community non-profits and not for sale**; Cooper and Lost Trail are **operating**. **Off-thesis or unavailable.**
- **Bluebird Backcountry (CO):** operated on **leased/permit** terrain and ceased operations in 2023. **There is no land asset to buy.** This one should be struck from the list entirely.

**`[INFERENCE]` Conclusion: the ex-ski-area category is the wrong asset class for this client.** These are distressed *ski businesses* — they come with lifts to recertify, base buildings, environmental liabilities, and often a permit dependency. The client's thesis (cheap land, near-zero OPEX, ranch/timber offset, private use first) points instead at **large deeded mountain ranches of 2,000–20,000+ ha in Montana, Wyoming, Idaho and Colorado**, where the alpine comes with the deed and the cattle/timber/hunting already pays the taxes.

### 3.4 Liability and insurance — quantified as best I can, with the key structural insight

`[INFERENCE]` **This is where the "private first" thesis pays off enormously, and it is the most valuable operational finding in this screen.**

There are two completely different regulatory animals:

**(a) Private, non-commercial — owner, family, invited guests, no consideration.** No paying public means: no state passenger-tramway licensing, no ANSI B77.1 lift inspection regime (if no public lifts), no ski-area statutory duty set, no commercial general-liability ski program. Exposure is covered under a farm/ranch policy with a personal umbrella. `[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]` State **recreational use statutes** further limit a landowner's duty to entrants who are not charged a fee — this protection is *lost the moment you charge*. Snowcat/snowmobile/ski-touring operation for the owner's own party sits squarely here. **OPEX for this mode is close to the client's target: property tax + a modest liability premium.**

**(b) Commercial or club — tickets, memberships, dues, paying guests.** You cross into: state passenger tramway board licensing (e.g. the Colorado Passenger Tramway Safety Board) and ANSI B77.1 for any lift; statutory ski-area operator duties (signage, marking, padding, boundary) under the state ski safety acts — **Colorado's Ski Safety Act 1979 (as amended)**, and the Montana, Utah, Idaho and Wyoming equivalents — which grant strong **assumption-of-risk / inherent-risk** defences in exchange for those duties; commercial GL from a thin specialist market (the ski-specific programs, e.g. MountainGuard and the Safehold/ski-insurance channel); workers' compensation; ski patrol with OEC-certified staff; and an avalanche mitigation program if terrain warrants.

`[INFERENCE]` **I could not obtain current premium quotations this session and will not guess a number.** What I will assert with confidence is the *shape*: US ski GL is a well-known hard, thin market and is one of the two or three largest fixed OPEX lines for a small area — this is precisely why so many small US areas have closed. **The client's staged plan is therefore exactly right, and the staging line is the paying-customer line, not the lift line.** Stay in mode (a) as long as possible. Model mode (b) only with a broker quotation in hand — this is a go/no-go input, not a detail.

### 3.5 Property tax — the low-OPEX lever

`[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]` The mountain states tax land in **agricultural or forest classification on productive earning capacity, not market value.** Colorado assesses agricultural land off a landlord-earnings capitalisation, producing per-acre taxes on grazing land that are typically a *very* small number of dollars per acre per year; Montana and Wyoming operate comparable agricultural/forest classifications, and **Wyoming has no state income tax.** `[INFERENCE]` This is the mechanism that makes a 5,000-hectare US mountain ranch cheaper to *hold* than a small suburban lot — and it is the reason the US survives the "keep OPEX extremely low" test despite the highest acquisition price in the screen.

**The trap:** classification is use-based. Land that stops being grazed or harvested can be **reclassified to residential/commercial at market value**, and a commercial ski operation will reclassify the portion it occupies. **Keep the cattle and the timber running** — they are not merely income offset, they are the tax shield. Also check **water rights** (prior appropriation; rights are severable and are a large part of ranch value) and **split estate / severed minerals** (a third party may own and be able to access the minerals under your mountain).

## 4. SCOTLAND — RANK 2

`[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]` **Scotland places no restriction whatsoever on foreign ownership of land.** A Malaysian individual or company may hold Scottish land outright. Since April 2022 the **Register of Persons Holding a Controlled Interest in Land (RCI)** requires disclosure of controlling interests — a *transparency* requirement, not a restriction.

**Why it ranks second:** `[INFERENCE]` Highland sporting estates of 2,000–20,000 ha routinely include multiple Munros (peaks over 3,000 ft / 914 m) **within the title**, and they are traditionally priced not per hectare but on the **stalking metric — a capital value per stag of annual cull.** That convention produces headline £/ha figures that are extraordinarily low for land containing 1,000 m summits: an estate of several thousand hectares carrying a modest stag cull has historically transacted in the low single-digit £millions. **This is, on a per-owned-peak basis, almost certainly the cheapest freehold alpine in the developed world.**

**Rural income is genuinely strong and diversified** `[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]`: red deer stalking, grouse, salmon and trout fishing, commercial forestry, and — the structural change of the last five years — **natural capital**: peatland restoration under the **Peatland Code** and afforestation under the **Woodland Carbon Code**, plus wind-farm option and lease income. `[INFERENCE]` Note the double edge: natural-capital demand has bid Scottish estate prices *up* sharply since ~2021, so the historic cheapness is eroding — but it also means the carrying cost can plausibly be fully covered.

**Why it does not rank first — two hard defeats:**

1. **Snow.** `[INFERENCE]` This is disqualifying for the core thesis. Scottish ski areas (Cairngorm, Glenshee, The Lecht, Nevis Range, Glencoe) operate between roughly 600 and 1,100 m in a maritime climate. Rain-on-snow at 900 m is normal, not exceptional; there have been repeated near-failed seasons; the Cairngorm funicular has been out of service for years. A private ski mountain here would be a **ski-touring estate with opportunistic seasons**, not a dependable operation.
2. **No exclusivity.** `[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]` The **Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003** confers a statutory public right of responsible access over essentially all land. **You cannot exclude the public from your mountain.** You may charge for lifts, guiding and accommodation, but the "private ski mountain" concept in its exclusive sense is not available.

**Emerging legal risk to check:** `[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]` the **Land Reform (Scotland) Bill (2024/25)** proposes prior-notification and **"lotting"** powers over transfers of large holdings (a threshold around 1,000 ha has been discussed), which could compel a large estate to be broken up and sold in lots rather than whole. **This directly threatens the acquisition of exactly the asset the client wants and must be checked for its final enacted form before any offer.** Also: deer management is regulated and NatureScot can compel culls.

## 5. SWEDEN — RANK 3

### 5.1 Acquisition is possible — the test is residence, not nationality

`[SECONDARY]` **jordförvärvslagen (1979:230)** requires *förvärvstillstånd* (acquisition permit) from the county administrative board (*länsstyrelsen*). Verified specifics:
- **The whole of Jämtland county, except certain designated *friområden*, counts as *glesbygd*** (sparse-settlement area), where a permit is required for a **natural person who has not been registered (*folkbokförd*) for the past year in that municipality's glesbygd area**.
- **Legal persons always require a permit** for glesbygd acquisitions.
- Application must be made to the länsstyrelsen **within three months** of the acquisition. **If no application is made, or the permit is refused, the acquisition is VOID.**
- The Act's stated purpose is to create employment and keep people living in sparse areas.

`[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]` Critically, **there is no nationality test.** Sweden repealed its foreign-acquisition control statute effective 1 January 1993 (SFS 1992:1368, repealing the 1982 Act on foreign acquisition of real property). A Malaysian *natural person* is assessed on the same criteria as a Swede: whether the acquisition promotes settlement and employment in the area.

`[INFERENCE]` **Structuring instruction: buy as a natural person, not through a company.** Swedish law treats *juridiska personer* far more restrictively — the Act materially constrains a legal person acquiring from a natural person, a rule designed to keep land in individual/family hands. A Malaysian corporate vehicle is the *worst* structure here; an individual with a credible plan to live on and use the property is the best.

### 5.2 The two defeaters — and they are severe

**(a) *Renskötselrätt* — the Sami reindeer husbandry right.** `[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]` This is the most important thing anyone can tell this client about Scandinavian mountain land, and it is routinely missed by foreign buyers.

Under **rennäringslagen (1971:437)**, the reindeer herding right is a **civil property right** vested in the Sami community (*sameby*), grounded in **immemorial prescription (*urminnes hävd*)** and constitutionally protected. It:
- **burdens all land within the reindeer husbandry area, including private freehold**;
- **is not extinguished by sale** — you buy the land subject to it, permanently;
- on ***åretruntmarker*** (year-round lands — effectively the entire fjäll region of Jämtland, Härjedalen, Västerbotten and Norrbotten west of the *odlingsgränsen*) confers rights to **graze, move herds, camp, take wood, fish and hunt**;
- **cannot be excluded by the landowner**, and materially constrains development that obstructs herding.

The **Girjas judgment (Girjas sameby v. the State, Swedish Supreme Court, NJA 2020 s. 3)** confirmed the strength of these rights, and Sweden's consultation regime (*konsultationsordningen*) requires consultation on measures affecting Sami interests.

`[INFERENCE]` **A ski operation is the archetypal conflicting use.** Snowcats, snowmobiles, groomed pistes, lifts and visitor volume disturb winter grazing precisely when reindeer are digging through snow for lichen. This is the single most litigated land-use conflict in Swedish mountain planning and is why Swedish resort expansions take a decade and get cut back. **A foreign buyer with no local political capital should assume the ski component will be obstructed.**

**(b) *Allemansrätten* — the right of public access.** `[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]` You cannot fence the public off your mountain. `[INFERENCE]` **This kills the exclusive private-club end-state in Sweden, Norway and Finland alike.**

### 5.3 Terrain and economics

`[INFERENCE]` Swedish fjäll offers roughly **300–800 m of vertical** (Åreskutan summit 1,420 m, ~890 m served vertical; Helags 1,797 m). Snow is cold, dry and reliable December–April, but the terrain is above treeline and wind-scoured, and **December–January daylight above 63°N is minimal** — a real constraint on a ski product. `[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]` Unproductive fjäll land (*impediment*, mire, mountain birch) trades at a small fraction of productive forest, so large hectarages are genuinely cheap; productive forest in the north is priced off standing volume (SEK per m³sk).

`[INFERENCE]` **Net: Sweden is the cheapest legally-clean large alpine title in the screen, and it is the one where you will least be able to do what the client wants with it.**

## 6. CANADA (BC / ALBERTA) — killed on tenure, but note one structure

`[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]`
- **The alpine is Crown land.** Fee-simple title containing genuine alpine summits barely exists in BC; the Kootenays have some high fee parcels but nothing at the scale and elevation the client needs.
- A commercial ski area requires a **Controlled Recreation Area** tenure under the **BC Commercial Alpine Ski Policy** (expression of interest → interim agreement → master development agreement). **This is a tenure over Crown land, not ownership.**
- **Cat-ski and heli-ski operations hold Commercial Recreation tenures** (typically licences of occupation) over very large Crown areas at low annual rent. They **are** bought and sold — but what changes hands is the *business plus the tenure*, and **assignment requires provincial consent**. `[INFERENCE]` I could not verify transaction prices this session.
- **Prohibition on the Purchase of Residential Property by Non-Canadians Act:** in force to **1 January 2027**. `[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]` It applies to **residential** property and, importantly, **does not apply to land outside census metropolitan areas and census agglomerations** — so remote rural BC/Alberta land is largely outside it. It is not the barrier here.
- **BC additional property transfer tax (20% foreign buyer tax)** applies only to **residential** property in specified regions (Metro Vancouver, Capital, Fraser Valley, Nanaimo, Central Okanagan) — **not** to remote rural land.

`[INFERENCE]` **Verdict: Canada is legally open to a Malaysian buyer for rural land, and closed by geology-of-tenure for alpine title.** It is the best *alternative structure* in the screen — if the client would accept operating rather than owning a mountain, a BC cat-ski tenure is the cheapest way to control huge alpine terrain anywhere. It simply is not the brief.

## 7. GEORGIA (COUNTRY) — killed

`[SECONDARY]` The Constitution in force from **16 December 2018** provides that **agricultural land may be owned only by the state, a self-governing entity, a citizen of Georgia, or a union of Georgian citizens.** Foreign individuals **and foreign-dominated companies** are prohibited. There is a narrow exception: **a Georgian-registered legal entity with foreign ownership may acquire agricultural land if the Government of Georgia approves it on the basis of an investment plan.** "Dominant partner" means >50% of shares. A 2019 draft law was passed at first reading to codify exceptional circumstances. One retrieved source (OC Media) refers to a *temporary lift* of the ban — **I could not open it and cannot state its scope or whether it remains in force.**

`[INFERENCE]` Kill, for three reasons: (1) the acquisition route is a **discretionary government approval**, not a right — the worst possible basis for a 20-year hold by a passive foreign owner; (2) the high alpine at Gudauri, Svaneti and Racha is **state land or communal pasture**, so even a successful company acquisition does not deliver the peaks; (3) **political and rule-of-law risk has deteriorated materially in 2024–26** (foreign-agents legislation, stalled EU accession, sustained unrest), against a background where Russia occupies roughly 20% of the country with occupation lines near mountain districts. Snow quality at Gudauri (2,200–3,300 m) is genuinely excellent — it is the only thing that recommends it.

## 8. TÜRKIYE — killed

`[SECONDARY]` Verified:
- **Reciprocity was abolished in 2012**; most nationalities may buy. `[INFERENCE]` **Malaysia is therefore eligible** — the barred list as of 2026 is Syria, Armenia and North Korea.
- **A foreign natural person is capped at 30 hectares nationwide**, and foreign acquisitions may not exceed **10% of the privately-owned land area of any district**.
- **Foreigners may not acquire property in prohibited military zones or military security zones**; acquisition in special/private security zones requires **governor's office** permission, and in military zones **General Staff** permission.

`[INFERENCE]` **30 hectares is 74 acres. That is fatal to the brief on its own.** A foreign-capital Turkish company can acquire under the Land Registry Law's Article 36 process and escapes the personal cap — but two further problems remain and they are structural: (a) under the Turkish Constitution and forest law, **state forest (*orman*) is inalienable**, and **common pasture (*mera*) cannot be privately owned** — and between them these two categories *are* the Kaçkar, Palandöken, Ilgaz and Munzur alpine zones; (b) the best terrain (Kaçkar, Erzurum, Ovacık/Munzur) lies in the north-east and east, where **military security zone clearance** is a live obstacle and, in the Munzur case, a long-running security problem. Snow at Palandöken (2,100–3,125 m) is good. It does not rescue the tenure position.

## 9. SOUTH-EAST EUROPE (Romania, Bulgaria, Bosnia, Montenegro, N. Macedonia, Albania, Kosovo) — killed

`[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]`
- **Romania:** Law 17/2014 bars **non-EU natural persons** from acquiring agricultural land outside built-up areas absent a treaty; a **Romanian-registered company (even wholly foreign-owned) may buy.** Law 175/2020 then added a ladder of **pre-emption rights** (multiple ranks of preferential purchasers), residency/registration seasoning requirements, and an **80% tax on resale within 8 years**. Forest land has a separate pre-emption regime. Private Carpathian forest exists at scale after restitution (Laws 1/2000 and 247/2005) — but above roughly 1,800 m the alpine pasture is held by **communal *obște* / *composesorat*** bodies and is not practically purchasable.
- **Bulgaria:** Constitution **Article 22** bars foreigners from acquiring land; EU citizens gained access post-accession; **non-EU (Malaysia) barred as a natural person**, with the Bulgarian-company workaround standard and effective. But **Rila and Pirin are national parks** — the peaks are not developable or ownable.
- **Montenegro:** foreign natural persons cannot own agricultural land above a small threshold, nor forest, nor land within ~1 km of the border; local company works. **Durmitor is a national park and UNESCO listed.**
- **North Macedonia: Šar Planina was declared a national park in 2021** — this removes the best Macedonian candidate from consideration.
- **Bosnia:** Bjelašnica/Igman terrain is largely state or under contested post-war title; Vlašić has private meadow.

`[INFERENCE]` **The killer across this whole region is not the law — the company workaround is routine — it is that you cannot assemble contiguous scale.** Post-socialist restitution and partible inheritance have fragmented mountain land into thousands of micro-parcels with incomplete cadastral records, absentee and emigrated co-owners, and unresolved restitution claims. Aggregating 2,000+ contiguous hectares of clean title would take years, dozens of separate negotiations, and would still leave title risk. Dinaric snowfall (Bjelašnica 2,067 m) is genuinely good and land is very cheap; neither compensates.

## 10. NORWAY, FINLAND, ICELAND, and the remaining "any other jurisdiction" set

`[PRIOR-UNVERIFIED]`

- **Norway — effectively closed.** *Konsesjonsloven* (2003) requires concession for most rural property above modest thresholds. **Boplikt** (personal residence obligation) attaches to built agricultural property and **driveplikt** (obligation to keep the land in production, 10 years) to farmland; **priskontroll** lets the authority refuse a price it considers too high. There is no nationality bar, but the regime is *designed* to prevent non-resident acquisition of large rural estates. Add *allemannsretten* and the fact that much high alpine is **statsallmenning** (state commons) or burdened by *beiterett*. **Kill.**
- **Finland — permit plus terrain failure.** The Act on the transfer of real estate requiring permission (**470/2019**, in force 1 January 2020) requires **Ministry of Defence permission for buyers from outside the EU/EEA** — so *every* Malaysian acquisition is a discretionary national-security screen. Decisive on its own, though: Finland's highest point is **Halti at 1,324 m**, and the fells deliver only 200–500 m of vertical (Ylläs ~463 m, Levi ~325 m). Reindeer herding rights cover the north. **Kill on terrain.**
- **Iceland — Act 19/1966** on the right of ownership and use of real property requires **Ministry of Justice permission for non-EEA persons**, tightened after the Grímsstaðir controversy. Add negligible forestry/ranch income, extreme wind, and frequent winter rain events. **Kill.**
- **Spain (Pyrenees) — the one worth a second look.** No nationality bar on freehold; large *fincas* of 1,000–5,000 ha exist in Huesca and Lleida reaching 2,000–2,800 m; **big-game hunting (ibex, chamois) is real income**; *monte* land is cheap. Two caveats: much land above treeline is ***monte de utilidad pública*** (municipal/communal), and the Pyrenees are a **border zone**, where non-EU acquisition of rustic land engages a military-authorisation requirement (Ley 8/1975) — routinely granted but a step. Sierra Nevada is national park. `[INFERENCE]` **This is the most under-screened credible candidate in the set and deserves the fourth slot of any follow-up.**
- **Italy / France — kill.** Italy applies the *condizione di reciprocità* to non-EU natural persons (Malaysia's own restrictive land regime makes reciprocity a live question), the abandoned *borghi* on the market are **valley villages, not alpine terrain**, and the surrounding tops carry *usi civici*. France applies **SAFER pre-emption** to rural transfers, and the *loi montagne* plus price levels defeat the low-cost thesis.
- **Armenia:** natural persons cannot own land; an Armenian legal entity can. Modest terrain, marginal and warming snow, and severe post-2023 border security risk. **Kill.**
- **Mongolia:** Land Law reserves **ownership to Mongolian citizens**; foreigners get possession/use rights only (60 years plus extension). Real alpine in the Altai. **Kill on tenure — confirmed leasehold-only as the brief suspected.**
- **Morocco (Atlas):** foreigners **barred from agricultural land**; the Atlas alpine is *terres collectives* or forest domain. **Kill.**
- **Lesotho:** all land is vested in the Basotho Nation; only leases (Land Act 2010, up to 90 years) with ministerial consent. Afriski at 3,222 m survives on machine-made snow. **Kill.**
- **Greenland:** **no private land ownership exists at all** — only allocated usage rights (*arealtildeling*). **Absolute kill.**
- **India / Nepal / Kashmir:** non-resident foreigners barred from agricultural and plantation land (India, FEMA); Nepal bars foreign land ownership outright. **Kill.**
