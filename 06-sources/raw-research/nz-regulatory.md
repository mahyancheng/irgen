## 0. Executive answer

A Malaysian private buyer **cannot realistically buy a NZ high-country station with meaningful alpine terrain in the title while remaining resident in Malaysia**, unless they can construct and deliver a *substantial, commercial, job-and-export-generating* farm/tourism plan that the Overseas Investment Office (OIO) accepts as making New Zealand *substantially better off than the current state*. A private ski mountain, by design, generates none of the things the farm land benefit test rewards.

There is exactly one clean structural alternative: the **"intention to reside in New Zealand indefinitely" consent pathway (s16 OIA 2005)**, which is not a benefit test — but its price is that the buyer must actually emigrate, become *ordinarily resident* (permanent resident visa + 183 days/yr in NZ + NZ tax resident) within 2 years, or be forced to sell.

Separately, and independently of the OIA: in the South Island high country, **the peaks are usually not in the title**. Tenure review systematically retired the alpine tops to the Department of Conservation (DOC) and freeholded the lower country. Roughly 300,000+ ha went to conservation; 346,000 ha (53% of land disposed) was freeholded, overwhelmingly the valley floors and mid-slopes.

## 1. Overseas Investment Act 2005, as amended by the Overseas Investment (National Interest Test and Other Matters) Amendment Act 2025 — in force 6 March 2026

### 1.1 Commencement
- The Amendment Act is **Public Act 2025 No 81**; commencement was set by the **Overseas Investment (National Interest Test and Other Matters) Amendment Act 2025 Commencement Order 2026 (SL 2026/2)**, bringing the regime into force **6 March 2026**. `[PRIMARY]`
- Accompanying **Overseas Investment Amendment Regulations 2026** were approved by Cabinet (LEG-26-MIN-0001). `[PRIMARY]`

### 1.2 What actually changed
- A **unified/consolidated national interest test (NIT)** replaces the separate investor test, benefit-to-New-Zealand test and standalone national interest assessment **for most assets** — significant business assets and *most* sensitive land. `[SECONDARY]`
- **Risk-based, two-stage process.** For screened assets other than farm land, residential land and fishing quota, **the regulator must grant consent within 15 working days unless there are reasonable grounds to consider a national interest risk exists.** Deeper scrutiny is reserved for national security, critical infrastructure and sensitive data. `[SECONDARY]`
- **Existing plantation forestry** is brought into the consolidated NIT. `[SECONDARY]`
- **New residential pathway:** from 6 March 2026, holders of certain **investor-class residence visas** may apply for OIO consent to buy or build **a single residential property valued at NZD 5 million or more**. `[SECONDARY]` — Note this is a *residential land* pathway; it does **not** open up farm land or large rural holdings.
- MBIE estimated ~NZD 5,650 of direct consenting cost savings and ~4 weeks off build timelines for the affected (residential) cases. `[SECONDARY]`

### 1.3 CONFIRMED: farm land, residential land and fishing quota were NOT changed
This is confirmed by multiple independent law-firm sources and is the single most important fact for this mandate:

> "The more onerous regime for farmland, fishing quota and residential land — and the farmland advertising regime — will not be changed. More specifically, the Government retained the 'Investor Test' and the 'Benefits to New Zealand Test' for farmland, fishing quota and residential land applications only."
> "For other assets, the investor, benefit to New Zealand and national interest tests are replaced by a consolidated national interest test … **but excluding farmland, fishing quota and residential land (which continue under current arrangements).**"
> "The reform introduces a fast-track consenting process for significant business assets and some sensitive land – **but not for residential land, farmland, and fishing quota.**"

`[SECONDARY]` — corroborated across Bell Gully, Minter Ellison, Russell McVeagh, Chapman Tripp, Anderson Lloyd, Buddle Findlay, Simpson Grierson, Hesketh Henry, PwC NZ.

**Consequence for this mandate:** the 2026 liberalisation is *irrelevant* to a high-country station purchase. A station is farm land. The buyer faces the **pre-2026, unreformed, most onerous** version of the regime — investor test **plus** the modified farm land benefit test **plus** the 30-working-day advertising regime **plus** the 100-working-day statutory clock. `[INFERENCE]`

## 2. "Sensitive land" — what triggers consent

Consent is required before an **overseas person** acquires an **interest in sensitive land** (Schedule 1, OIA 2005). Relevant categories:

| Trigger | Threshold |
|---|---|
| **Non-urban land** (i.e. not both in an urban area *and* used for commercial/industrial/residential purposes) | **> 5 hectares** |
| Foreshore and seabed | any |
| Land held for conservation purposes under the Conservation Act 1987 | > 0.4 ha |
| Land **adjoining** a reserve under the Reserves Act 1977 administered by DOC | > 0.4 ha |
| Land adjoining a lake or a park | > 0.4 ha |
| Land adjoining the foreshore | > 0.2 ha |

`[PRIMARY]` (Schedule 1 Tables 1 & 2, OIA 2005 — as reported by LINZ guidance and NZ Law Society practice briefing; **statutory text not read directly** `[UNVERIFIED]` as to exact wording).

**Key mechanics:**
- **"Interest in land" includes a leasehold interest of 3 years or more, including rights of renewal.** `[PRIMARY/SECONDARY]` — this is why acquiring a **Crown pastoral lease** is itself a consented transaction (see §7).
- **Associated land aggregation (s 8(4)):** land owned/controlled by the applicant or an associate that adjoins, or is on the same island, is aggregated when testing the 5 ha threshold. You cannot subdivide your way under the threshold. `[PRIMARY]`
- **"Farm land"** = land used exclusively or principally for agricultural, horticultural or pastoral purposes, or for keeping bees, poultry or livestock. `[PRIMARY]`
- **"Special land"** = foreshore, seabed, riverbed or lakebed. If sensitive land being acquired includes special land, it must be **offered to the Crown**. The 2025 Amendment moved the obligation to make that offer from the vendor to the overseas person, so it can be dealt with *after* consent, removing a historic source of delay. `[PRIMARY/SECONDARY]`

**For a station purchase, essentially every trigger fires at once:** thousands of hectares of non-urban land (>5 ha), almost certainly adjoining conservation land or a DOC reserve, frequently adjoining a lake, and frequently containing riverbed. `[INFERENCE]`

## 3. The farm land benefit test — the crux

### 3.1 The seven benefit factors (s 17 OIA 2005)
1. **Economic benefits** — creation/retention of jobs, introduction of technology or business skills, increases in productivity or **export receipts**, reduced risk of illiquid assets, increased processing of primary products.
2. **Benefits to the natural environment** — protection of indigenous flora and fauna, erosion control.
3. **Continued or enhanced public access** within or over the sensitive land (including recreational access, stewardship, kaitiakitanga).
4. **Continued or enhanced protection of historic heritage** — heritage covenants, Heritage NZ Pouhere Taonga Act 2014 actions, Māori reservation.
5. **Giving effect to or advancing a significant Government policy.**
6. **Oversight of, or participation in, the investment by persons who are not overseas persons** (i.e. New Zealanders).
7. **Other consequential benefits to New Zealand.**

`[PRIMARY]` (s 17, OIA 2005).

### 3.2 The farm land uplift — how it differs from the general test
Changes effective **24 November 2021** (Overseas Investment Amendment Act 2021, Public Act 2021 No 17) created a **"modified benefit to New Zealand test"** for acquisitions of estates or interests in **farm land exceeding 5 hectares**:

- The relevant Ministers **must give the economic benefits factor and the oversight-or-participation-by-New-Zealanders factor HIGH RELATIVE IMPORTANCE.** `[PRIMARY]`
- The applicant must demonstrate, **in relation to one or more of those two high-importance factors**, that the benefits are of a **size or nature that represents a substantial benefit to New Zealand**. `[PRIMARY]`
- The benefits must be **substantially higher than the current state**. `[PRIMARY]`
- **The counterfactual changed in 2021.** The old counterfactual (compare against what a hypothetical alternative purchaser might do) was **removed**. The comparator is now **the existing state of affairs / current state** of the land. `[SECONDARY]` (Russell McVeagh; Buddle Findlay)
- **Asymmetric treatment of negatives:** the regulator can generally weigh negative impacts only against *directly comparable* aspects of the *same* factor (jobs lost vs jobs gained), not across factors (jobs lost vs technology gained). `[SECONDARY]`

### 3.3 What this means in practice
`[INFERENCE]` The two factors that carry the weight are the two a private ski buyer is worst placed to satisfy:
- **Economic:** you must show *more jobs, more exports, more productivity, more primary-product processing* than the station produces today. A station that runs 8 staff and exports wool and store lambs sets a real baseline. Cutting stock numbers to make room for a private ski operation runs the *wrong way* on the high-importance factor and, because the negative is directly comparable (jobs vs jobs, export receipts vs export receipts), it can be netted off against you.
- **Oversight/participation by New Zealanders:** a 100%-owned private mountain has essentially zero NZ participation. A joint venture with a NZ partner, NZ directors, and NZ management is the only lever here — and it must be real, not cosmetic.

### 3.4 Decision precedents (high-country stations)

**Hunter Valley Station (Otago, ~6,468 ha, Lake Hāwea / Hunter Valley).** Consent granted to **Orange Lakes (NZ) Ltd** (US beneficial owner, Matt Lauer). `[SECONDARY]` (ODT, NZ Herald, LINZ)
- The OIO recorded the buyer would **continue to farm sheep and cattle** and would **invest capital to significantly enhance production**. Estimated capital value NZD 13.2m.
- **Conditions imposed** turned heavily on **public access**: commitments to support improved access to Lake Hāwea and the Kidds Bush campsite, **legalising the road to the DOC Kidds Bush campsite**, and supporting an **easement over the Sawyer Burn Track**, plus western access to Sentinel Peak.
- On Hunter Valley Station Road itself the OIO accepted continued **"informal access"** as sufficient, with the owners required to "act reasonably". Only **2 of 9** Walking Access Commission recommendations were adopted — which triggered public backlash and threatened litigation.
- **Note:** the transaction was an acquisition of the **lease** (a Crown pastoral lease); LINZ confirmed "the owner of the land remains the Crown". `[PRIMARY]` (LINZ news release)

**Mt White Station (Canterbury, ~39,337 ha Crown pastoral lease + ~678 ha freehold, Bealey / near Arthur's Pass).** Acquired via **Southern Ranges Ltd** by Czech-born **Lukáš Trávníček**, a NZ permanent resident who had temporarily returned to the Czech Republic and so was an "overseas person" at the time. `[SECONDARY]` (RNZ, NZ Herald, Newsroom)
- **He applied under the residency ("intention to reside") pathway, NOT the benefit test.**
- RNZ reported explicitly: under that pathway applicants "**are not required to show benefits to New Zealand, including walking access, so the OIO was not in a position to make access a condition of consent.**"
- Conditions: **return to New Zealand within just over 12 months** and **cease to be an overseas person within 2 years**; failing which, **dispose of the property**.

**Motatapu / Mount Soho / Glencoe / Coronet Peak stations (Shania Twain / Soho Properties).** `[SECONDARY]` (ODT, Herenga ā Nuku)
- The **29 km Motatapu Track** was a condition of OIO approval; 23 walking/cycling tracks were built or upgraded.
- **QEII National Trust covenants** were later placed over most of the four stations, protecting landscape, flora/fauna and heritage **and opening areas for public recreational access** (walking, mountain biking).

**Porters (Canterbury ski area).** In 2006 the OIO approved a NZD 1.7m acquisition by an Australian-dominated consortium; four Russian investors put in NZD 6.9m in 2011 as part of an expansion plan. The operation involved **708 ha leased from DOC**, and a controversial proposed land swap (198 ha of conservation land at Crystal Basin to be freeholded in exchange for a 70 ha Banks Peninsula forest block). `[SECONDARY]` (Newsroom, Wikipedia)

**Pattern `[INFERENCE]`:** Where consent is granted on the **benefit** pathway for a high-country property, **public access is the currency the OIO extracts**. Every benefit-pathway station approval in the record above came with access obligations — tracks, easements, road legalisation, covenants. This is directly adverse to a buyer whose thesis is a *private* mountain.

## 4. The farm land advertising requirement

- Before an overseas person can **enter into a transaction** for farm land, the land must have been **offered for acquisition on the open market to persons who are not overseas persons**, in accordance with the Regulations. `[PRIMARY]`
- **Minimum 30 working days** of advertising. `[SECONDARY]` (PwC NZ; LINZ guidance)
- **Prescribed form:** advertisements must be **both online and in a print publication**, and must fall within specified timeframes. `[SECONDARY]` — *the precise prescribed wording/format sits in the Overseas Investment Regulations 2005 (advertising provisions were inserted by the Overseas Investment Amendment Regulations 2018). I could not read the regulation text.* `[UNVERIFIED]`
- **Purpose:** ensure New Zealanders get first chance to buy farm land.
- **Exemptions:** Ministers may exempt a person or transaction where **the cost of compliance is unduly burdensome**. `[SECONDARY]` A **2024 Gazette notice** created **class exemptions**. In practice exemptions have been granted for **leasehold acquisitions of farm land for solar farms** (first in 2022, several since). An exemption application should generally be made **before** the consent application. `[SECONDARY]` (Simpson Grierson; LINZ)
- **Practical effect for this mandate `[INFERENCE]`:** a station is unambiguously farm land, and there is no visible precedent for exempting a *private recreational* buyer. The buyer must expect to compete on the open market for 30 working days, and to be the *underbidder-of-record* explanation if a New Zealander was outbid. This also removes any ability to do a quiet off-market deal — which is precisely how most trophy high-country properties change hands.

## 5. Would a private, low-intensity ski/recreation plan score well or badly?

**Conclusion: badly — materially worse than a commercial tourism plan, and probably fatally on the farm land test.** `[INFERENCE]`, reasoned from the statutory factors above:

| Factor | Weight for farm land | Private ski mountain | Commercial ski/tourism development |
|---|---|---|---|
| Economic benefits (jobs, tech, exports, processing, productivity) | **HIGH** | Near zero. Possibly *negative* if stock is reduced. No exports. No processing. Maybe 1–3 caretaker roles. | Strong: construction jobs, permanent operational jobs, international visitor spend (counts toward export receipts as services exports), tourism supply chain. |
| Oversight/participation by New Zealanders | **HIGH** | Near zero unless a genuine NZ JV/board is built. | Can be engineered: NZ operating company, NZ directors, NZ management team, NZ minority equity. |
| Natural environment | Normal | Can be made credible: wilding conifer control, tussock retirement, predator control, QEII covenant. Cheap, and genuinely valued. | Same, plus ability to fund it. |
| **Public access** | Normal | **Actively contradicted by the thesis.** The OIO's revealed preference (Hunter Valley, Motatapu) is to extract access. Offering none is a visible negative. | Access is inherent to a commercial field; scores well. |
| Historic heritage | Normal | Neutral / opportunistic (musterers' huts, station heritage). | Same. |
| Advance significant Government policy | Normal | Weak. | Moderate — tourism growth and regional development are stated policy. |
| Other consequential benefits | Normal | Weak. | Moderate. |

**The structural problem:** the farm land test is not a "do no harm" test. It requires a **substantial benefit** under one of the two high-importance factors, **substantially higher than the current state**. A private mountain is, by construction, a *reduction* in economic activity relative to a working station. Environment and heritage benefits — the categories where a low-impact private owner genuinely shines — are explicitly **not** the high-importance factors for farm land. `[INFERENCE]`

**The one credible benefit-pathway construction `[INFERENCE]`:** present it as a **farming business intensification plus commercial alpine tourism venture** — retain/expand the sheep-and-beef operation with real capex and headcount, add a *commercially operated* (i.e. paying-public) cat-ski / heli-ski / ski-touring / lodge business run through an NZ-managed operating company with NZ minority equity, and add environmental and public-access sweeteners. The private-use benefit is then a by-product of a genuinely commercial enterprise. This is a fundamentally different business to the one in the thesis, with a fundamentally different OPEX/CAPEX profile.

## 6. Consent pathways for a non-resident foreign natural person

### 6.1 Who is an "overseas person"
- Any individual who is **not a NZ citizen and not ordinarily resident in NZ**. `[PRIMARY]`
- Any entity **incorporated overseas**, or in which overseas persons hold **25% or more ownership or control interest**. `[PRIMARY]`
- **25% or more ownership or control interest** = beneficial entitlement to / interest in ≥25% of securities; **or** power to control the composition of ≥25% of the governing body; **or** the right to exercise or control ≥25% of the voting power at a meeting. `[PRIMARY]`
- Trusts, bodies corporate and partnerships are all caught on the same 25% test. `[PRIMARY]`

**Structural consequence `[INFERENCE]`:** you cannot cure the problem by putting an NZ company on the title. If the Malaysian buyer holds ≥25% of that company (economically, by board control, or by votes), the company *is* an overseas person and the transaction is screened exactly as if the individual bought it. A genuine JV in which the buyer holds **<25%** on all three limbs would fall outside the regime — but that means holding a minority stake with no board control, which is not ownership of a private mountain in any meaningful sense. Nominee/sham arrangements are directly targeted by the Act's enforcement provisions and by "control" tests; do not attempt this.

### 6.2 "Ordinarily resident in New Zealand" — the four-part test
1. Holds a **residence class visa** — and, following the 2018 amendment, for the relevant purposes a **permanent resident visa** (a plain resident visa is *not* enough); `[SECONDARY]` — *the precise interaction between "residence class visa" and "permanent resident visa" across land classes could not be verified from statutory text* `[UNVERIFIED]`
2. Has **resided in New Zealand for the past 12 months**;
3. Has been **present in New Zealand for at least 183 days of the past 12 months**;
4. Is a **New Zealand tax resident**.

`[PRIMARY/SECONDARY]` (LINZ guidance).

**Note the tax consequence `[INFERENCE]`:** limb 4 makes NZ tax residence a *precondition* of escaping the regime. For a Malaysian buyer this means exposure to NZ worldwide income tax (top personal rate 39%), which is a material and often decisive cost. Dentons has flagged that the new 2026 residential consent pathway "could create tax residency risk" — the same logic applies with far more force here.

### 6.3 The "intention to reside in New Zealand indefinitely" pathway — **s 16 OIA 2005**
This is the single most important structural finding.

- **s 16** provides that one criterion for consent for overseas investments in **sensitive land** is that the relevant overseas person (or each individual with control of it) is a **NZ citizen, ordinarily resident in NZ, OR intending to reside in New Zealand indefinitely**. `[PRIMARY]`
- This pathway supports overseas persons **moving to New Zealand to make it their home**, where the land is **sensitive land that is not residential land**. `[PRIMARY]` (LINZ guidance)
- Applicants are expected to hold a **residence class visa or entrepreneur work visa** and to provide **evidence of activities, plans and supporting evidence showing an intention to live permanently in NZ within 12 months**. LINZ is explicit: **"a simple assertion that you intend to remain indefinitely is not likely to be enough."** `[PRIMARY]`
- **Conditions if granted:** move to NZ within **12 months** of consent, and become **ordinarily resident within 2 years** of consent. **If not satisfied, LINZ may require you to sell the land.** `[PRIMARY]`
- **This pathway does not require a benefit case.** RNZ, reporting the OIO's own position on Mt White Station: applicants under this pathway "**are not required to show benefits to New Zealand, including walking access, so the OIO was not in a position to make access a condition of consent.**" `[SECONDARY]` — *this is the load-bearing citation for the whole structure and should be re-verified against the Act and current LINZ guidance by NZ counsel.*
- **Mt White Station is the live precedent:** ~40,000 ha (39,337 ha pastoral lease + 678 ha freehold) of Southern Alps high country, consented on this pathway with return-in-12-months / not-an-overseas-person-in-2-years / else-dispose conditions. `[SECONDARY]`

**Open question `[UNVERIFIED]`:** whether the **farm land advertising requirement** still applies to a purchase made under the intention-to-reside pathway. Search results were explicit that they could not confirm this. The advertising obligation appears to attach to the *land type* (farm land) rather than to the *consent pathway*, which would suggest it does still apply — but a class or individual exemption may be available. **Get a written LINZ view before committing.**

### 6.4 Active Investor Plus (AIP) visa
- Two categories: **Growth** — invest **NZD 5m** in acceptable higher-risk growth assets for **36 months**, with a **21-day** NZ presence requirement over the period; **Balanced** — invest **NZD 10m** in a broader mix for **60 months**, with a **105-day** presence requirement. `[SECONDARY]`
- **From 1 June 2026**, Growth-category applicants may allocate **up to 20% of the total investment to philanthropic contributions**. `[SECONDARY]`
- No Expression of Interest stage — direct application. Partners and dependent children can be included; the presence requirement applies to the principal applicant. `[SECONDARY]`
- It is a **residence** pathway and can lead to permanent residence. `[SECONDARY]`

**Critical caveats `[INFERENCE]`:**
1. **AIP does not buy the land.** The AIP investment must go into *acceptable investments* — direct investments in NZ businesses, managed funds, philanthropy. **Buying a station is not an acceptable AIP investment.** The NZD 5m/10m is *in addition to* the purchase price.
2. **AIP's low presence requirement (21 days over 3 years) is deliberately incompatible with "ordinarily resident" (183 days per year).** An AIP holder who spends 21 days in NZ is still an **overseas person** for OIA purposes. AIP alone therefore does *not* solve the land problem; it only supplies the *visa* limb needed to make an intention-to-reside application credible — and then the buyer must go far beyond the AIP presence minimum to satisfy the 2-year ordinarily-resident condition.
3. The new **6 March 2026 residential pathway** for investor-visa holders (single property ≥ NZD 5m) is **residential land only** and is of no use for a station.

### 6.5 Timeframes and fees
- **Statutory assessment timeframe for farm land: 100 working days** — the longest of any category. Can be **paused or extended** by the OIO. `[PRIMARY]` (LINZ)
- April 2024 Ministerial Directive Letter directs the OIO to decide **80% of applications within half the statutory timeframe** → ~**50 working days** target for farm land. `[SECONDARY]`
- **These timeframes create no legally enforceable obligation and there is no recourse if missed.** `[PRIMARY]`
- **Fees:** current schedule **effective 16 March 2026**. Reported figures: **NZD 22,800** for a stage-one primary consent assessment; a further **NZD 83,700** if a national interest assessment is required; overall filing fees quoted in the range **NZD 33,600 – 141,900** depending on type and complexity. `[SECONDARY]` — **the specific farm land / sensitive land benefit-test application fee could not be isolated** `[UNVERIFIED]`. Budget NZD 100k+ in OIO fees and legal costs as a planning assumption, plus the cost of the advertising campaign and (if sought) a separate exemption application fee.
- **Realistic elapsed time `[INFERENCE]`:** 30 working days advertising (~6 weeks) **before** you can even contract, then up to 100 working days (~5 months) of OIO assessment with pause rights. **Six to twelve months from decision to settlement is a reasonable planning assumption**, and the vendor must be willing to hold the deal open that long.

## 7. Crown pastoral leases

### 7.1 Crown Pastoral Land Reform Act 2022
- Ended the **tenure review** process **the day after Royal assent — 18 May 2022**. Only reviews already at *substantive proposal* or *implementation* stage could continue. `[PRIMARY]`
- Introduced a **new consenting process for discretionary pastoral activities**. `[PRIMARY]`
- Introduced a **new public access consideration**: when the Commissioner of Crown Lands (CCL) assesses **lease transfers and sublease applications**, the CCL must consider whether the applicant has **unreasonably refused requests for public access** over the land in the past. `[PRIMARY]`
- Further changes took effect **17 November 2022**; new regulations and a new **Commissioner of Crown Lands' standard** came into force **September 2023**. `[PRIMARY]`

### 7.2 What a pastoral lessee has
- **Exclusive possession and quiet enjoyment**, and the **right to graze**. Ownership-like rights over improvements — fencing, tracks, buildings. `[PRIMARY]` (LINZ)
- **33-year term with a perpetual right of renewal** — on expiry the lessee is entitled to a new 33-year lease on the same conditions at a revised rent. In substance, **exclusive possession in perpetuity** subject to compliance. `[PRIMARY]`
- **Rent reviewed every 11 years**, assessed on **the land's stock-carrying capacity for pastoral farming**. `[PRIMARY]` — *this is an important commercial feature: rent is tied to pastoral productive capacity, not to tourism or amenity value.* `[INFERENCE]` It is a very cheap carrying cost for a very large area, which is exactly why the thesis is attractive — **and equally why the Crown retains tight control over any non-pastoral use.**
- Created in the 1940s–50s under the **Land Act 1948**; administered by LINZ. `[PRIMARY]`

### 7.3 What a lessee may NOT do without consent
- **The land may only be used for pastoral farming.** `[PRIMARY]`
- Permitted without consent: grazing; exotic pest plant control; **minor** soil disturbance; activities within the existing curtilage of dwellings. `[PRIMARY]`
- **Requires CCL consent (discretionary pastoral activities):** soil disturbance, burning vegetation, **constructing buildings and infrastructure**, forestry, and **commercial recreation activities**. `[PRIMARY]`
- **The CCL must consult the Director-General of Conservation** before deciding a discretionary pastoral activity, and must consider the **adverse effects** of the activity and the **inherent values** likely to be affected. `[PRIMARY]`

### 7.4 Recreation permits — the ski-field mechanism
- **"The Commissioner can grant easements over pastoral land, and recreation permits for certain non-pastoral commercial activities, such as tourism, hunting, ski fields or filming."** `[PRIMARY]` (LINZ guidance) — **explicit confirmation that a ski field on a pastoral lease is a separate CCL recreation permit, not a lease right.**
- Recreation permits are granted under **s 66A Land Act 1948** and must meet **LINZ Standard LINZS45002** (Standard for purchase, alienation and administration of Crown land), with permit terms drawn from **Appendix H** of that Standard. `[PRIMARY]`
- LINZ publishes individual **commercial recreation permit decisions** (e.g. A6171831 multiple recreational activities; A6142892 hiking/MTB/horse trekking/4WD/accommodation/camping; A7687668 short-term accommodation in existing buildings; several filming and adventure-race permits). Typical conditions restrict **vegetation removal, soil disturbance, fire lighting, and building structures**, and require **land restoration**. `[PRIMARY]`
- **Permit term lengths and fee basis could not be established** `[UNVERIFIED]` — LINZ publishes the decisions but the search did not surface durations or royalty structures. LINZ contact: pastoral@linz.govt.nz.

### 7.5 Transferability
- **Transfer requires CCL consent**, and since the 2022 Reform Act the CCL must weigh the applicant's history of refusing public access. `[PRIMARY]`
- Subleases likewise require consent. `[PRIMARY]`
- **Independently, an overseas person acquiring the lease needs OIO consent** because a leasehold interest of ≥3 years (incl. renewals) is an interest in land, and a pastoral lease is farm land. Both Hunter Valley Station and Mt White Station were OIO-consented **lease** acquisitions. `[PRIMARY/SECONDARY]`

### 7.6 **Does a pastoral lease give the lessee rights to develop ski infrastructure? — NO.**
`[INFERENCE]`, but strongly supported:
- The lease confers **grazing** rights, not development rights.
- **Buildings and infrastructure are discretionary activities** requiring CCL consent, with mandatory DOC consultation and an adverse-effects/inherent-values test.
- **Commercial recreation, including ski fields, requires a separate s 66A recreation permit.**
- Published permit conditions routinely **prohibit constructing structures** and restrict soil disturbance and vegetation removal.
- Therefore: **a pastoral lease is a licence to graze a mountain, not a right to build a ski area on it.** Any lift, lodge, snowmaking pond, or new road on pastoral leasehold requires CCL consent (in addition to RMA resource consent and Building Act consent), and the CCL must first consult DOC. A buyer paying a premium for "skiable terrain" on a pastoral lease is buying an *option that the Crown can decline*.
- **Private (non-commercial) skiing** by the lessee and guests sits in a grey zone: it is not "pastoral" but it may not be "commercial recreation" either. `[UNVERIFIED]` — the boundary is not documented in anything I could retrieve. **This must be put to LINZ in writing before acquisition.** The safest reading is that non-commercial recreation by the lessee within existing tracks and buildings is tolerated, and that anything involving paying guests, new structures, new tracks, or soil/vegetation disturbance is not.

## 8. Why alpine tops are usually NOT in the title

### 8.1 The tenure review pattern
Tenure review (Crown Pastoral Land Act 1998, run 1998–2022) split a pastoral lease into two parts:
- **Lower/more productive country → freeholded** to the lessee (often with a discounted price and sometimes cash to the lessee);
- **High-altitude land with "significant inherent values" → retired to full Crown ownership as public conservation land**, managed by DOC.

`[PRIMARY/SECONDARY]`

### 8.2 The numbers
- Since 1998, **346,000 ha freeholded** through tenure review — **53% of the total land disposed of**. `[SECONDARY]` (Newsroom; Wolters Kluwer; Victoria University *Policy Quarterly*)
- By implication, **~300,000+ ha went the other way, into the conservation estate.** `[SECONDARY]`
- **Five new parks were created** from land added to the conservation estate. `[SECONDARY]`
- Crown pastoral leases fell from **303** at the passage of the Crown Pastoral Land Act to **171 remaining**, covering **1.2 million hectares — nearly 5% of New Zealand.** `[SECONDARY]`

### 8.3 What this means for a buyer wanting peaks in the title
`[INFERENCE]`:
1. **Freehold high-country titles created by tenure review are systematically topped out.** The freehold boundary was drawn at the point where "significant inherent values" (alpine tussock grassland, snow tussock, scree, cushionfield, alpine herbfield, tarns) began — which is exactly the elevation band where skiable terrain begins. A tenure-reviewed freehold station will typically own the fans, terraces, faces and mid-slopes and stop below the tops.
2. **Conversely, land that was NOT tenure-reviewed is still a pastoral lease** — where the tops may be *within the lease* but, per §7.6, come with no development rights and no security of use for skiing.
3. **The two large stations in the record confirm this.** Hunter Valley Station (6,468 ha) is a **lease** — the Crown remains owner. Mt White Station is **39,337 ha lease + only 678 ha freehold** — i.e. **98.3% leasehold**.
4. **Therefore the search should be narrowed to a specific and rare object:** a station whose *freehold* title (not lease) extends above the treeline and over a peak — typically a pre-1998 freehold that never went through tenure review, or a tenure review where the alpine land had low ecological values (e.g. heavily modified, wilding-infested, or a rain-shadow scree face). These exist but are a small subset. **Verify against the LINZ record title and the DOC estate boundary before any offer — never against a marketing map.** `[INFERENCE]`

## 9. DOC concessions for ski areas

### 9.1 Legal basis
- **Part 3B, Conservation Act 1987** ("Concessions"). `[PRIMARY]`
- A concession may take the form of a **lease, licence, permit or easement**. Ski areas are typically granted as a combined **lease and licence**. `[PRIMARY]`
- Applications are **publicly notified** and open to submissions (DOC "Have your say" consultations: NZSki Ltd 2022 and 2023; Mount Cheeseman Ski Club Inc 2025; Tekapo Ski Field Ltd 2019). `[PRIMARY]`

### 9.2 Terms
- Reported: **a lease or licence term must not exceed 30 years**, unless there are **exceptional circumstances**, in which case not more than **60 years**. `[PRIMARY]` — **caution:** the source framing referenced a decision-making *panel*, which suggests this specific formulation came from **Schedule 6 of the Fast-track Approvals Act 2024** rather than the standard Conservation Act pathway. The 30/60 split is consistent with long-standing Conservation Act practice, but **verify which limb applies** `[UNVERIFIED]`.
- Real-world examples: **The Remarkables — 40-year term**; **Mount Cheeseman Ski Club — 30 years**. `[SECONDARY]`
- **Mt Dobson** (Mackenzie, at the southern end of Te Kāhui Kaupeka Conservation Park) is marketed as holding "**a long-term licence from the Department of Conservation for exclusive concession on prime alpine land**" — and the field has been **offered for sale**. `[BROKER-CLAIM]` (mtdobson.co.nz; SnowsBest)
- **Porters** involved **708 ha leased from DOC**. `[SECONDARY]`
- **Craigieburn Forest Park** (DOC-administered) contains **Broken River** and **Craigieburn Valley** ski areas; **Temple Basin** is likewise a club field on conservation land. `[SECONDARY]`
- **Ōhau** and **Tekapo/Roundhill** are also on or adjacent to conservation land. `[SECONDARY]`

### 9.3 Assignability, renewal risk, and rent
- **Assignability and renewal terms could not be verified.** `[UNVERIFIED]` Search results explicitly noted that Part 3B's assignment/renewal provisions were not retrievable. `[INFERENCE]` NZ concessions are ordinarily assignable **only with the Minister's/Director-General's written consent**, and renewal is generally **not** an automatic right — the operator applies afresh and DOC may re-notify. This is the standard structure for Crown concessions and is consistent with the notified-application record above, but it must be confirmed.
- **Rent/royalty basis could not be verified.** `[UNVERIFIED]` `[INFERENCE]` DOC concession rentals for commercial recreation are typically a market-based fee, often a percentage of gross turnover with a minimum annual rental.

### 9.4 **Concessions are not ownership**
`[PRIMARY/INFERENCE]` A DOC concession is a time-limited, conditional, publicly-notified permission to occupy and use land the Crown owns. It:
- **does not confer title** and cannot be mortgaged as freehold;
- is **terminable for breach** and subject to renewal risk at expiry;
- **carries public participation risk** — every application and material variation is notifiable and attracts submissions;
- is **valued as a wasting asset**, which materially affects both financing and exit.

**Consequence for the thesis `[INFERENCE]`:** almost every *existing* NZ ski area you might contemplate acquiring is sitting on Crown land under a concession. That is the opposite of "the legal title itself includes genuine skiable terrain and peaks." Buying a NZ ski area generally means buying a **business plus a concession**, not a mountain.

## 10. Planning / RMA

### 10.1 Status of RMA reform (2026)
- The Government introduced **two replacement Bills in Parliament in December 2025**: the **Natural Environment Bill** (use, protection and enhancement of the natural environment) and the **Planning Bill** (use, development and enjoyment of land). `[PRIMARY/SECONDARY]`
- The **Environment Select Committee heard public submissions through February and March 2026**. `[SECONDARY]`
- The Government intends to **pass both into law by mid-2026**. `[SECONDARY]`
- Predecessors: the Natural and Built Environment Act 2023 and Spatial Planning Act 2023 were **repealed** by the Resource Management (Natural and Built Environment and Spatial Planning Repeal and Interim Fast-track Consenting) Act 2023. `[PRIMARY]`
- **As at the date of this note the RMA 1991 remains the operative statute, with replacement imminent.** `[INFERENCE]` — **This is a live transition risk.** Any consenting strategy built on current district plan provisions could be disrupted by the National Direction/Planning Act transition. Confirm current status before relying on any plan rule.

### 10.2 Queenstown Lakes District Plan (QLDC) — the clearest ski-specific regime
- The Rural Zone classifies landscape into **Outstanding Natural Landscapes (ONL)**, **Outstanding Natural Features (ONF)**, and **Rural Character Landscapes**. ONL/ONF protection is a **matter of national importance under s 6 RMA**. `[PRIMARY]`
- **Ski Area Sub-Zones** sit **within** the Rural Zone. Their stated purpose is "**to enable the continued development of Ski Areas as year round destinations for ski area, tourism and recreational activities within the identified Sub-Zones.**" `[PRIMARY]` (PDP Chapter 21 Rural)
- **Critically: "Identified Ski Area Sub-Zones are EXCLUDED from the Outstanding Natural Feature, Outstanding Natural Landscape and Rural Character Landscape categories applied to the balance of the Rural Zone, and from the policies of this chapter related to those categories."** `[PRIMARY]`
- All Rural Zone rules apply within the Sub-Zones unless stated to the contrary. `[PRIMARY]`

**This is the single most important planning fact for the mandate `[INFERENCE]`:** in Queenstown Lakes, **ski development is enabled inside mapped Ski Area Sub-Zones and confronts full ONL/ONF protection outside them.** A buyer acquiring rural alpine land that is **not** in a Ski Area Sub-Zone is proposing development in an ONL — where landscape effects are a matter of national importance, activity status will be at best discretionary and realistically **non-complying** for structures, and where the plan's policies are protective rather than enabling. Extending or creating a Ski Area Sub-Zone requires a **private plan change**, which is a multi-year, publicly notified, appealable process with no assurance of success.

### 10.3 Mackenzie District Plan
- Provisions from **Plan Change 13 (PC13)** apply primarily to **Te Manahuna / the Mackenzie Basin Outstanding Natural Landscape**, and have been integrated into a district-wide **Natural Features and Landscapes (NFL)** chapter in the district plan review. `[PRIMARY]`
- Minimum site areas: **20 ha** in the Eastern Plains Special Control Area; **100 ha** in the General Rural Zone where **not** identified as being within an ONL. `[PRIMARY]` — implying materially tighter control inside the ONL.
- Plan Changes 23–27 were notified in 2023–24. `[PRIMARY]`
- **The Mackenzie District Plan's specific ski-area rules could not be retrieved.** `[UNVERIFIED]` Note that **Mt Dobson, Roundhill/Tekapo and Ōhau** are in or near this district.

### 10.5 What consent would each activity need?
`[INFERENCE]` — reasoned from the general structure of the RMA and the ONL framework above; **not verified against specific plan rules**:

| Activity | Likely requirement |
|---|---|
| **Ski touring / private snowmobile / snowcat on existing tracks, no structures, no earthworks** | Under **s 9 RMA**, land use consent is required only where a **rule in a plan** says so. Informal recreation over farmland typically has no rule against it, so it is likely **permitted** in a Rural General zone. **Risk points:** vegetation-clearance and earthworks rules (regional and district) if tracks are formed or widened; noise rules; and any ONL-specific rules on vehicles/tracks. |
| **Snowcat *operating base*, workshop, fuel storage** | Building consent under the **Building Act 2004**; likely a **land use consent** if a new building in an ONL; hazardous substances (fuel) rules. |
| **Surface lift (rope tow / platter / T-bar)** | It is a **structure** and involves **earthworks and foundations**. In an ONL, expect **discretionary or non-complying**. Also **PECPR Regulations 1999** (see §14). |
| **Chairlift** | Same as above but with far greater landscape/visual effect — in an ONL outside a Ski Area Sub-Zone, realistically **non-complying**, requiring the s 104D gateway to be passed (effects no more than minor **or** not contrary to plan objectives and policies). |
| **Lodge / accommodation** | Land use consent (visual amenity, ONL), **Building Act** consent, on-site wastewater discharge consent from the **regional** council, water supply, and potentially a district plan "visitor accommodation" activity classification. |
| **Snowmaking pond and water take** | **Regional** council water permit (take) and land use consent (damming/diversion, bed disturbance), plus discharge permit for pond desilting. `[PRIMARY]` — recorded that NZ snowmaking consents include "water abstraction and discharge permits for desilting within snowmaking storage ponds". |
| **New access road / benched track above the snowline** | Earthworks and vegetation-clearance consents (district and regional), landscape assessment in an ONL. |

**"Is private/non-commercial recreation a permitted activity in Rural General zones?"** `[INFERENCE]` — **Probably yes as an activity, but that is the wrong question.** The RMA regulates *effects*, not commerciality. What triggers consent is **building, earthworks, vegetation clearance, water take and discharge** — all of which a ski operation needs, and all of which are tightly controlled in an ONL regardless of whether anyone is charged a lift ticket. The "private, low-key" framing buys you very little planning relief; it buys you *procedural* relief only in the sense that a smaller footprint has smaller effects.

### 10.6 Environmental evidence base against alpine ski development
- DOC science publications (SFC085, SFC120A) document the effects of **snow grooming and skiing on fragile alpine vegetation**: "**tussock plants can be compressed and decapitated by grooming and skiing wherever the snow pack is thin**"; soil and vegetation disturbance by over-snow vehicles decreases as snowpack deepens. `[PRIMARY]`
- Three of the four main downhill fields in west Otago (**Coronet Peak, The Remarkables, Treble Cone**) are on **DOC land**, and concerns have been raised about impacts on alpine vegetation. `[PRIMARY]`
- `[INFERENCE]` This body of DOC-published evidence will be cited by submitters against any alpine consent application, including for snowcat grooming.

## 12. Holding costs

### 12.1 Rates
- NZ councils rate on **capital value (CV)** and/or **land value (LV)**, and must **revalue at least every three years**. `[PRIMARY]` (QV / councils)
- **Mackenzie District:** total rateable value reached **NZD 6.3 billion**; land value of those properties **NZD 3.5 billion**; **pastoral capital values +18%** in the most recent revaluation. `[SECONDARY]` (QV / Stuff)
- **Central Otago District:** 16,070 properties revalued to 1 September 2025; total rateable value **NZD 23.84 billion**, up **23.8%**; **pastoral values +29%**. `[SECONDARY]` (QV / ODT)
- **A specific rates figure for a high-country station could not be obtained.** `[UNVERIFIED]` `[INFERENCE]` High-country stations are usually low-rated relative to area because their CV per hectare is very low, but note the strong recent CV inflation (+18% to +29% on pastoral) will feed directly into rates. **Get the actual rates assessment for any target property — it is a public record obtainable from the council rating database (e.g. CODC "Rating and Property Search").**
- **Otago Regional Council** levies separate regional rates. `[PRIMARY]`

### 12.2 QEII covenants
- An **open space covenant** is a **perpetual** legal agreement between QEII National Trust and the landowner, **registered against the title**. Covenants **"run with the title" and bind all future owners — buying a property with a covenant means the new owner is automatically bound.** `[PRIMARY]`
- The covenant spatially defines the protected area, specifies the values protected, and lists **prohibited actions** (e.g. burning forest, draining wetland). `[PRIMARY]`
- The main establishment cost is **fencing for stock exclusion**, usually shared **50:50** with QEII after third-party contributions (QEII pays up to **60%** for deer fencing). QEII monitors every **2–3 years**. `[PRIMARY]`
- `[INFERENCE]` **Two-sided relevance:** (a) an existing QEII covenant over the alpine part of a target property is a **hard, perpetual constraint** on any ski infrastructure and must be searched on title before offer; (b) **offering a new QEII covenant is one of the cheapest and most credible "benefit to the natural environment" sweeteners** an OIO applicant can put on the table — and it was precisely the mechanism used on the Motatapu/Soho stations.

### 12.3 Significant Natural Areas (SNAs)
- The **National Policy Statement for Indigenous Biodiversity 2023 (NPS-IB)** came into force **4 August 2023**, requiring councils to identify SNAs and give effect to the NPS. `[PRIMARY]`
- The **Resource Management (Freshwater and Other Matters) Amendment Act 2024** inserted a new **s 78** suspending the "give effect as soon as reasonably practicable" duty in relation to **clause 3.8(5)** (the SNA identification requirement) and certain other provisions **for a 3-year period**. `[PRIMARY]`
- The NPS-IB has **not been repealed**, and remains subject to change under the broader RMA reform. `[SECONDARY]`
- `[INFERENCE]` **The SNA mapping pause is temporary and expires around 2027.** A buyer should assume that alpine indigenous vegetation on any target property will eventually be mapped as an SNA under whatever regime succeeds the NPS-IB, and that SNA status will further constrain earthworks, vegetation clearance and structures.

## 13. Public access

- **Walking Access Act 2008.** Purpose: provide the public with **free, certain, enduring and practical walking access to the outdoors**. It established the **New Zealand Walking Access Commission / Herenga ā Nuku Aotearoa**. `[PRIMARY]`
- **Marginal strips** — the land along the coast, river banks and lake margins retained by the Crown when it granted or sold land; the major part of what is colloquially called the "Queen's Chain". Usually **20 metres wide**; can be reduced if a lesser width still gives good access. Marginal strips created under the **Conservation Act 1987 are ambulatory** — they move if the river, lake edge or sea margin moves. `[PRIMARY]`
- **There is no general right of public access across private land.** Owners have an inherent **right of exclusive occupation and enjoyment**, enforceable under the Trespass Act. `[PRIMARY]` — *the search synthesis cited "Trespass Act 1990"; the NZ statute is conventionally cited as the **Trespass Act 1980**. Treat the year as unverified.* `[UNVERIFIED]`
- **So: yes, a private freehold owner can exclude the public from alpine terrain within the title** — subject to (a) any marginal strips, (b) any registered easements, public roads or unformed legal roads ("paper roads") crossing the land, (c) any QEII or OIO-imposed access covenant, and (d) for **pastoral leasehold**, the CCL's new statutory duty to consider a lessee's history of unreasonably refusing access when deciding transfers and subleases. `[INFERENCE]`
- **The OIO access lever.** Under the benefit test, the OIO "must take into account whether there are or will be adequate mechanisms in place for providing, protecting or providing walking access over the relevant land by the public." Public access is the *only* "public benefit" factor available, and it is presumed to be delivered via a **Walking Access Act 2008 agreement**. `[PRIMARY/SECONDARY]` (Herenga ā Nuku)
- `[INFERENCE]` **A buyer who acquires on the benefit pathway should expect to be asked to give up permanent, registered public access — and the record (Motatapu 29 km track; Hunter Valley easements and road legalisation) shows the OIO does extract it.** A buyer who acquires on the intention-to-reside pathway is (per RNZ's report of the OIO's position on Mt White) **not** subject to access conditions. That asymmetry is a further, and large, argument for the residency route.

## 14. Health and safety, avalanche and liability

### 14.1 ACC and the bar on suing
- New Zealand's **no-fault Accident Compensation** scheme covers everyone in NZ injured in an accident, including recreational injury. `[PRIMARY]`
- Because of the no-fault basis, **people who suffer personal injury have no right to sue for compensatory damages** — the exception is **exemplary damages**. `[PRIMARY]`
- `[INFERENCE]` **This is a genuine and material advantage of New Zealand over almost every other jurisdiction for a private ski operation.** The catastrophic US-style skier-injury liability exposure essentially does not exist. What remains is **regulatory** exposure (WorkSafe prosecution and fines) and **exemplary damages** in egregious cases — not compensatory tort.

### 14.2 HSWA 2015 and landowners
- **WorkSafe's operational policy is explicit:** "**HSWA only applies to recreational access when the land is affected by a PCBU's work activities or is part of a workplace. This means a PCBU whose land is being accessed for recreation is only responsible for risks arising from the work or workplace, and is not responsible for the risks associated with the recreational activities.**" `[PRIMARY]`
- Separately, a **PCBU who manages or controls a workplace** must ensure, so far as reasonably practicable, that the workplace, its means of entry and exit, and anything arising from it are without risks to the health and safety of **any person**. `[PRIMARY]`
- **Landowners who allow access to their land for adventure activities may have duties under HSWA**; what is required "will depend on the circumstances of access, the risks arising from the land, and the nature of the activity." `[PRIMARY]` (WorkSafe guidance for land owners/managers)

`[INFERENCE]` **The dividing line for this mandate:**
- A landowner who **merely permits** friends/guests to ski at their own risk on land that is a *farm* workplace is responsible for **farm hazards** (e.g. an unfenced offal pit, a hidden culvert, machinery), **not** for the avalanche risk inherent in the skiing.
- A landowner who **operates** the skiing — grooms runs, runs a snowcat with a driver, provides guiding, runs a lift, or charges — is a **PCBU conducting that undertaking**, and owes the full primary duty of care to workers *and* to other persons put at risk. The avalanche risk then becomes **their** risk to manage so far as is reasonably practicable.
- **The moment you put a paid driver in a snowcat carrying guests, you are a PCBU running a work activity.** "Private" does not switch HSWA off; **employing or engaging anyone** switches it on.

### 14.3 Adventure Activities Regulations
- **Health and Safety at Work (Adventure Activities) Regulations 2016 (LI 2016/19).** PCBUs providing an adventure activity must be **registered with WorkSafe** and must **pass a recognised safety audit** against the **Safety Audit Standard** (published by Gazette notice 2023-au3976). `[PRIMARY]`
- **Exclusions:** activities run by **sports clubs (or associations representing sports clubs)** and **schools** in most circumstances; **registered schools and tertiary education providers**; and — importantly — **activities where participants are not taught or guided**. `[PRIMARY]`
- **Whether commercial snow-sports guiding / cat-skiing is a scheduled "adventure activity" could not be confirmed.** `[UNVERIFIED]` `[INFERENCE]` Guided backcountry skiing and heli/cat-skiing are widely understood in NZ to fall within the regime; the "not taught or guided" exclusion is the operative distinction. **A club-field structure is worth serious examination**, because the club/association exclusion is explicit in the regulations and the Craigieburn/Broken River/Temple Basin club fields are the living NZ precedent for a low-cost, low-regulation, member-operated ski area.

### 14.4 Lifts — passenger ropeways
- Governed by the **Health and Safety in Employment (Pressure Equipment, Cranes and Passenger Ropeways) Regulations 1999** ("PECPR Regulations"). `[PRIMARY]`
- Covered installations: **aerial ropeways** (chairlifts, gondolas, jig-back tramways) **and surface ropeways** (**T-bars, platter lifts and rope tows**) — "the establishment, maintenance and overall safety of these uphill facilities." `[PRIMARY]` (**Approved Code of Practice for Passenger Ropeways in New Zealand, 1998 edition**, WorkSafe)
- Inspections must comply with the PECPR Regulations and **NZS 8635:2018 (Safety requirements for cableway installations)**. `[PRIMARY]`
- Inspectors performing "specified activities" must hold **certificates of competence**; **CBIP** is the WorkSafe-recognised Qualification Issuing Agency. `[PRIMARY]`

`[INFERENCE]` **Important cost/complexity finding for the thesis:** the phrase "simple surface lifts" does **not** map to "unregulated" in New Zealand. **Rope tows and nutcracker tows are passenger ropeways** and fall inside the PECPR regime, with design, registration, certified inspection and NZS 8635 compliance obligations. The regulatory step-change is not between "surface lift" and "chairlift" — it is between **"no lift at all"** (snowcat / touring / snowmobile, outside PECPR) and **"any lift"** (inside PECPR).

### 14.5 Avalanche standards
- The **NZ Guidelines and Recording Standards for Weather, Snowpack and Avalanche Observations (2017)**, published by the **New Zealand Mountain Safety Council**, are the national standard. `[PRIMARY]`
- The **New Zealand Avalanche Advisory** (avalanche.net.nz) is the national forecasting service. `[PRIMARY]`
- **New Zealand Certificate in Avalanche Risk Management** is an NZQA-listed qualification. `[PRIMARY]`
- Professional guiding qualifications: **NZMGA** (mountain guides), **NZOIA Alpine**. `[PRIMARY]`
- **SAANZ (Ski Areas Association of New Zealand)** exists and issues operating protocols/planning templates to member ski areas. `[SECONDARY]` — **SAANZ's safety standards, avalanche-control requirements and any audit regime could not be verified.** `[UNVERIFIED]` `[INFERENCE]` SAANZ is an **industry association**, not a regulator; membership is voluntary and its standards are not law. Compliance obligations flow from HSWA, the Adventure Activities Regulations and PECPR, not from SAANZ.

### 14.6 Insurance
- **Not researched.** `[UNVERIFIED]` `[INFERENCE]` With ACC removing compensatory personal-injury claims, the insurance requirement is materially narrower than in the US/Europe: statutory liability (defence costs and, where insurable, fines), property/material damage on remote alpine assets, plant and machinery (snowcats are expensive and hard to insure remotely), business interruption, and employers' liability gap cover. Get a NZ broker quote early — remote alpine property cover is not commodity business.

## 15. Synthesis: the least-bad structures, ranked

`[INFERENCE]` throughout this section.

**A. Emigrate — the "intention to reside" pathway (s 16 OIA 2005).** The only route that avoids the farm land benefit test, avoids public-access conditions, and permits a genuinely private use. Precedent: Mt White Station, ~40,000 ha. Cost: the buyer must obtain a residence class visa (AIP is the practical route: NZD 5m Growth / NZD 10m Balanced, *separate from* the land price), move to New Zealand within 12 months of consent, become **ordinarily resident within 2 years** (permanent resident visa + 183 days/yr + **NZ tax residence**), or be forced to sell. **This is an emigration decision dressed as a property transaction.** The farm land advertising requirement probably still applies `[UNVERIFIED]`.

**B. The commercial-venture benefit case.** Buy on the benefit pathway with a plan that is genuinely commercial: farming intensification with real capex and headcount, plus a paying alpine tourism operation, run through an NZ-managed operating company with meaningful NZ equity and NZ directors, plus environmental (QEII covenant, wilding control, predator control) and **public access** commitments. Expect access easements to be extracted as a condition. This works — but it is not the thesis; it is a real business with real OPEX.

**C. Genuine minority JV (<25% on all three limbs).** Falls outside the regime entirely, but the buyer holds no control. Only viable with a deeply trusted NZ partner and a carefully drafted shareholders' agreement that gives economic and use rights without triggering the "control" tests. **High risk of being characterised as control; take specialist advice; do not use nominees.**

**D. Do not buy pastoral leasehold expecting to build.** A pastoral lease is a perpetually-renewable, cheap-rent licence to **graze**. Buildings, infrastructure, soil disturbance and commercial recreation all need Commissioner of Crown Lands consent, with mandatory DOC consultation. Ski fields specifically require a **s 66A Land Act 1948 recreation permit**. Leasehold is fine for the *ranching* leg of the thesis and terrible for the *ski infrastructure* leg.

**E. Do not buy an existing NZ ski area expecting to own a mountain.** Most sit on Crown land under DOC concessions (Part 3B Conservation Act 1987) — time-limited, non-assignable without consent, publicly notifiable, renewal-risked, and **not ownership**.

**F. Consider the club-field model for the operating layer.** The Adventure Activities Regulations 2016 expressly exclude activities run by clubs and associations representing clubs. Craigieburn, Broken River and Temple Basin are the working NZ precedent for a low-cost, low-regulation, member-run alpine operation. This does not solve the land problem, but it is the cheapest lawful operating wrapper once the land is held.

**G. Sequence the risk.** Get an OIO/planning opinion **before** offering. In particular: (i) pull the LINZ record title and confirm exactly what the freehold covers versus what is pastoral lease versus what is DOC estate; (ii) search the title for QEII covenants, marginal strips, easements and unformed legal roads; (iii) confirm district plan zoning and whether any Ski Area Sub-Zone or equivalent exists; (iv) get LINZ's written view on whether the private, non-commercial recreation you intend is within the pastoral lease's permitted uses; (v) confirm whether farm land advertising applies to your pathway.
