## 1. CHILE — FOREIGN OWNERSHIP: THE HEADLINE ANSWER

**A Malaysian non-resident can own Chilean rural land in fee simple (*dominio pleno*),
including Andean land on the Argentine border, with no nationality-based restriction and
no government approval.** This is the strongest single finding in this workstream, and it
is the point on which Chile beats most alpine jurisdictions.

### 1.1 The general rule

Chilean law contains no general restriction on non-resident foreigners acquiring real
estate. `[SECONDARY]` Ley 20.848 (published 25 June 2015, in force 1 January 2016, the
Foreign Direct Investment framework that replaced DL 600) provides that foreign investors
are governed by **the same legal regime applicable to nationals, without arbitrary
discrimination**, with rights of non-discrimination and of remitting capital and net
profits. `[PRIMARY/SECONDARY]`

Note for structuring: the Ley 20.848 *certificate* regime (access to the formal FX
market, remittance guarantees) attaches to capital transfers of **USD 5 million or more**.
`[SECONDARY]` A sub-USD-5m land purchase simply does not use that regime — it is an
ordinary purchase. It does **not** become restricted; it just sits outside the FDI
certificate framework. `[INFERENCE]`

### 1.2 DL 1939 of 1977, Article 6 — the 10 km / 5 km rule (does NOT bite here)

Article 6 provides that **fiscal (State-owned) land** located up to **10 km from the
border** may only be obtained in property, lease or any other title by Chilean natural or
legal persons. The same rule applies to **fiscal land within 5 km of the coast**, measured
from the high-tide line — though there exceptions may be granted to foreigners *domiciled
in Chile* with a prior favourable report from the Subsecretaría de Marina (Ministry of
National Defence). `[PRIMARY]`

**This is the decisive distinction for the thesis.** Article 6 governs the State's
*disposal of its own land*. It is a constraint on what CONAF/Bienes Nacionales may sell or
lease to a foreigner, **not** a constraint on the private conveyancing market. Buying an
existing privately-titled *fundo* whose boundary runs to the Argentine frontier does not
engage Article 6 at all. `[INFERENCE — high confidence, but see §14 verification list]`

### 1.3 DL 1939 of 1977, Article 7 — border zones (applies only to neighbours)

Article 7 prohibits **nationals of bordering countries** — Argentines, Bolivians and
Peruvians — from acquiring *dominio* or other real rights, or exercising possession or
mere tenancy, over real estate situated wholly or partly in zones declared *zonas
fronterizas* under **DFL No. 4 of 1967** of the Ministry of Foreign Relations. `[PRIMARY]`

Scope details:
- It catches **both fiscal and private property** in declared border zones. `[PRIMARY]`
  (This is the trap people assume applies to everyone. It does not.)
- It catches legal entities with principal seat in a bordering country, **or with 40% or
  more of capital held by their nationals, or under their effective administration and
  control**. `[PRIMARY]` — a real structuring constraint if Argentine partners are
  contemplated.
- It operates on an express **logic of reciprocity**: it applies to nationals of bordering
  countries where equivalent prohibitions exist against Chileans. `[PRIMARY]`
- The President may exempt a bordering-country national by *decreto supremo fundado* on
  national-interest grounds, for specified properties. `[PRIMARY]`

**"For nationals of other countries, this limitation does not exist, even if the property
is in a border zone."** `[PRIMARY/SECONDARY — vLex; Cámara de Diputados committee doc]`

### 1.4 Conclusion for a Malaysian buyer

| Instrument | Bites a Malaysian? |
|---|---|
| Ley 20.848 (FDI) | No — grants equal treatment |
| DL 1939 art. 6 (10 km border / 5 km coast) | **No** — applies to *fiscal* land transfers only |
| DL 1939 art. 7 (border zones, DFL 4/1967) | **No** — nationality-limited to AR/BO/PE |
| Ley 19.253 art. 13 (indigenous land) | Only if the parcel is *tierra indígena* — see §5 |

Chile therefore offers something rare: **freehold to the summit, on the international
frontier, to a non-resident Asian buyer, with no consent regime.** Compare New Zealand
(Overseas Investment Act consent for "sensitive land" — effectively all rural land over
5 ha) and Argentina (§10). `[INFERENCE]`

## 2. CHILE — BUYING MECHANICS

All `[SECONDARY]` (Chilean law-firm and conveyancing sources; not verified against SII or
Conservador primary text).

- **RUT is mandatory.** Notaries and Conservadores de Bienes Raíces are *obliged* to
  require a Chilean tax ID. A foreigner can obtain a **RUT for specific purposes**
  (including buying real estate) **without residency**.
- **No residency and no Chilean company are required.** A natural-person non-resident may
  hold title directly.
- **Process:** private *promesa* (optional) → *escritura pública de compraventa* signed
  before a *notario* → *inscripción* in the Registro de Propiedad of the competent
  **Conservador de Bienes Raíces**. Title vests on inscription.
- **Timeline:** inscription typically **1–3 weeks** after signature. (Rural, multi-*rol*
  or unsurveyed properties will be slower — `[INFERENCE]`.)
- **Remote purchase is possible** via a *poder notarial especial* to a Chilean mandatary:
  must be by *escritura pública*, **apostilled** (Malaysia is not a Hague Apostille party
  — verify; consular legalisation may be required `[INFERENCE]`) and *protocolizado*
  before a Chilean notary.
- **Costs:** notary fees are customarily borne by the buyer; Conservador inscription fees
  are ad valorem. Annual *impuesto territorial* applies; capital gains income tax applies
  on future sale; VAT can apply to certain transactions (habitual vendors / new build).
  **I could not retrieve a fee schedule** — the commonly cited rough order is well under
  1% of price for notary + Conservador combined, but that is `[MODEL-KNOWLEDGE —
  UNVERIFIED]` and must be quoted by a Chilean notary.
- **There is no stamp duty on real-estate conveyance in Chile** (the *impuesto de timbres
  y estampillas* attaches to credit documents, i.e. mortgages, not to the sale itself).
  `[MODEL-KNOWLEDGE — UNVERIFIED — verify with counsel; if correct this is a meaningful
  cost advantage over NZ/AU/JP]`

### 2.1 Title risk — the real Chilean rural problem
`[INFERENCE from the legal framework retrieved; not separately searched]`

Chile's Conservador system is a *registro de inscripciones*, not a State-guaranteed
Torrens register. Rural Andean *fundos* frequently exhibit:
- **títulos no saneados** — chains broken by unregistered inheritances (*posesión
  efectiva* never completed), giving apparent sellers no clean title;
- boundaries described by natural features ("hasta la línea de altas cumbres") with no
  modern survey, so the *cabida* (stated hectares) is nominal;
- overlapping *deslindes* with neighbours and with fiscal land above the treeline.

**Standard mitigation:** a 10-year *estudio de títulos* by a Chilean lawyer plus a
*plano de deslindes* by a surveyor, both as conditions precedent. Budget for the stated
hectarage to be wrong. This is not a Chile-specific dealbreaker but it is a real diligence
cost and it is where a cheap headline price per hectare quietly goes.

## 3. CHILE — WATER RIGHTS (Código de Aguas, as reformed by Ley 21.435)

Ley 21.435 was **published 6 April 2022**. `[PRIMARY]` Key features for a land buyer:

- **New rights are 30-year concessions.** "El derecho de aprovechamiento que se origina en
  una concesión será de treinta años", granted according to criteria of **availability of
  the source and/or sustainability of the aquifer**. `[PRIMARY]`
- **Automatic renewal, defeasible for non-use.** The duration "se prorrogará por el solo
  ministerio de la ley y sucesivamente, **a menos que la Dirección General de Aguas
  acredite, mediante una resolución fundada, el no uso efectivo del recurso** o que existe
  una afectación a la sustentabilidad de la fuente que no ha podido ser superada."
  `[PRIMARY]` — i.e. renewal is the default; the DGA bears the burden of proving non-use.
  This is materially less alarming than the headline "perpetual rights abolished".
- **Registration duty for pre-existing rights.** Rights obtained before Ley 21.435 must be
  registered in the **Catastro Público de Aguas** by the *titular* (via the DGA website or
  form). `[PRIMARY — Chileatiende/DGA]`
- **Deadlines and the moving target:** the original 6 April 2025 deadline was extended to
  **6 April 2027** by Ley 21.586 (2023) and **Ley 21.727 (February 2025)**. `[PRIMARY]`
  Extinction of rights *not inscribed in the Registro de Propiedad de Aguas* applies from
  **6 April 2025**. `[PRIMARY]` As of **April 2026** a bill is in Congress proposing
  extension to **2030**, on the stated ground that the DGA and the registries still lack
  capacity to process the backlog. `[SECONDARY — Diario Constitucional, 14 Apr 2026]`

### 3.1 What this means for the acquisition
`[INFERENCE]`
1. **Water rights do not automatically pass with the land.** They are a separate real
   right with a separate registry. A *fundo* sold "with water" may have rights that are
   unregistered, unperfected, or extinguished. **Make DGA/Catastro verification a
   condition precedent.** Given the 2025–2027 extinction mechanics, a seller with
   unregistered rights is a live risk, and the pending 2030 bill means the risk window is
   politically unstable.
2. **For the thesis specifically, this matters less than it looks.** Stock water and lodge
   domestic water are small consumptive uses. **Snowmaking is the exposure**: it is a
   large seasonal consumptive abstraction, is exactly the kind of use the DGA now assesses
   against "disponibilidad de la fuente", and in the central Andes (33–36°S) under drought
   conditions a new snowmaking right is likely to be difficult or unobtainable. A thesis
   built on *natural* snow and low CAPEX is well aligned with the reformed Code; a thesis
   that later needs snowmaking is not.
3. The reform's `no uso efectivo` caducidad favours a buyer who **actually uses** water
   (stock, lodge) over a speculator — consistent with the client's operating model.

## 5. CHILE — INDIGENOUS LAND (Ley 19.253)

`[PRIMARY]` Ley 19.253 (published 5 October 1993), **Article 13**:
- Land recognised as *tierra indígena* **cannot be alienated, encumbered, or acquired by
  prescription, except among communities or indigenous persons of the same ethnicity**.
- Land owned by indigenous communities **cannot be leased, lent, or ceded to third parties
  in use, enjoyment or administration**.
- CONADI may authorise alienation by resolution of the Director **inserted in the
  instrument**, subject to reimbursement to the Fund of the value of any subsidy, credit or
  benefit received, CPI-updated.
- **Breach produces *nulidad absoluta* of the act or contract.** `[PRIMARY]`

**Where this bites `[INFERENCE]`:** the prohibition is a **status of the parcel**, not a
geographic zone. It therefore does not blanket-prohibit purchase in Araucanía — but in
Araucanía, Los Ríos and parts of Biobío, where *tierras indígenas* and CONADI-purchased
land are dense, the probability that a given *fundo* (or a strip within it) is affected is
materially higher, and the sanction is total nullity of the purchase, not damages.

**Distinct and separate risk:** *de facto* land conflict and *tomas* (occupations) in the
Araucanía/Arauco macrozone, and the political process of land restitution via CONADI's
Fondo de Tierras. **I ran no searches on the current security situation or on the status of
the state of emergency in the macrozona sur, and cannot characterise it.** This is a
first-order risk for any Araucanía target (which includes Malalcahuello/Lonquimay/Corralco,
one of the client's named leads) and must be researched before that zone is shortlisted.

**Practical rule:** obtain a **CONADI certificate of non-indigenous character** for every
*rol* as a condition precedent, in any region south of the Biobío.

## 6. CHILE — PLANNING, ENVIRONMENT AND FOREST LAW

### 6.1 SEIA — and a very low threshold for ski centres `[PRIMARY]`

DS 40/2012 (Reglamento del SEIA), **Article 3, literal g.2** defines *proyectos de
desarrollo turístico* as building and urbanisation works permanently destined for lodging
and/or tourism equipment — expressly including **"centros o canchas de esquí"** — which
meet at least one of:

| Trigger | Threshold |
|---|---|
| (a) Built surface | ≥ **5,000 m²** |
| (b) Predio surface | ≥ **15,000 m² (1.5 ha)** |
| (c) Simultaneous capacity / flow | ≥ **300 persons** |

Projects listed in art. 10 Ley 19.300 and detailed in art. 3 of the Reglamento must enter
the SEIA; those with art. 11 effects require a full **EIA** rather than a **DIA**.
`[PRIMARY]`

**This is the most consequential planning finding, and it cuts both ways `[INFERENCE]`:**

- Threshold (b) — **1.5 hectares of predio surface** — is trivially exceeded by any real
  ski area. So **any commercial ski development enters the SEIA.** Plan for a DIA at
  minimum, with an EIA if native forest, glaciers, wetlands, or indigenous communities are
  affected (art. 11 effects).
- **But** the definition is anchored on **"obras de edificación y urbanización"**. A
  *private*, non-commercial operation using **existing roads**, snowcats, snowmobiles,
  ski-touring and portable/temporary surface tows, with **no permanent building or
  urbanisation works**, arguably does not constitute a *proyecto de desarrollo turístico*
  at all and falls outside art. 3 g.2 entirely.
- **This is precisely the client's Phase 1 thesis, and Chilean law appears to accommodate
  it.** The strategic implication: **the SEIA is the wall between Phase 1 (private) and
  Phase 2 (commercial)** — not a barrier to entry. Cost of crossing it is real (a DIA is
  typically 6–12 months; an EIA considerably longer) but it is deferred until demand
  justifies it, exactly as the thesis contemplates.
- ⚠️ **Verify via a *consulta de pertinencia*** to the SEA (a formal, cheap, published
  procedure — the `pertinencia.sea.gob.cl` documents surfaced in search are exactly these).
  Do this *before* buying, describing the intended Phase 1 operation. This is the single
  highest-leverage pre-acquisition regulatory step available in Chile.

### 6.2 Native forest — Ley 20.283 `[PRIMARY]`

- **All cutting of native forest requires a *plan de manejo* previously approved by
  CONAF.**
- CONAF must approve or reject **within 90 days**; **silence = deemed approved**
  (positive administrative silence — unusually favourable).
- A *plan de manejo* is valid for a **maximum of 15 years**.
- Cutting of *bosques nativos de conservación y protección* requires technical
  justification of methods plus measures for soil, water quality and quantity,
  biodiversity and fire prevention.
- **Cutting of native trees and shrubs is prohibited within 500 m of glaciers.** `[PRIMARY]`
- **Penalties:** unauthorised cutting attracts a fine of not less than the commercial value
  of the cut products, up to double; where cutting is unauthorised the penalty equals
  **double the commercial value, with a minimum of 5 UTM per affected hectare**.

**Implication for piste cutting `[INFERENCE — and this is central to the thesis]`:**
1. **Terrain above the treeline is not *bosque* and Ley 20.283 does not reach it.** A
   property whose skiable terrain lies above the *Nothofagus*/*Araucaria* line can be
   developed for skiing without engaging the forest law at all.
2. Below the treeline, cutting pistes through native forest is **not prohibited but is
   permitted-and-planned** — a CONAF *plan de manejo* with a 90-day deemed-approval clock.
   That is far more workable than an outright ban, but it is a per-project consent with
   real ecological conditions.
3. The **500 m glacier buffer** is a hard geographic constraint that will matter on the
   volcanic peaks (Chillán, Lonquimay, Villarrica, Longaví all carry glaciers).
4. **Araucaria araucana is a Natural Monument with its own protective regime** —
   `[MODEL-KNOWLEDGE — UNVERIFIED]` — which, if correct, effectively forecloses piste
   cutting in the Araucaria belt of Lonquimay/Malalcahuello/Conguillío. **Verify.** This
   would materially damage the Araucanía leads.

⇒ **Design conclusion:** target a property whose *ski* value is above treeline and whose
*forest* value is a separate, lower, commercially-managed asset. This simultaneously
maximises regulatory freedom and preserves the forestry income leg of the thesis.

**12.2 Snow, by latitude (orientation only).**
- **33°S (Farellones/Valle Nevado/Portillo):** highest elevations (bases ~2,400–3,000 m+),
  but the epicentre of the post-2010 megadrought; high inter-annual variance; land near
  Santiago is *expensive*, not cheap. Poor fit for a cheap-land thesis.
- **36°S (Nevados de Chillán, Longaví, Antuco):** the plausible sweet spot — substantially
  wetter than 33°S, volcanoes reaching 2,700–3,200 m, treeline ~1,600–1,800 m giving a real
  above-treeline band, land materially cheaper than Santiago's cordillera, and no Araucanía
  security overlay. **If I had budget for one zone, this is where I would look first.**
- **38°S (Lonquimay/Malalcahuello/Corralco, Villarrica, Sollipulli):** excellent snowfall
  and volcanic fall-line terrain, but (a) *Araucaria araucana* protection may foreclose
  piste work, (b) much of the best terrain is inside Reservas Nacionales (CONAF), not
  freehold, and (c) the Araucanía land-conflict and Ley 19.253 overlay is at its most
  acute. High geographic quality, high legal/political friction.
- **45°S (Aysén/Coyhaique):** cheap land, reliable snow, but low elevation, heavy maritime
  weather, very short daylight in mid-winter, and severe access/logistics. Fits "cheap
  land + ranch" but not "operable ski mountain".

**12.3 Chilean ski tenure precedents (orientation only).** Chilean ski areas commonly sit
on a **mix** of private freehold and **concessions over fiscal land or National Reserve
land administered by CONAF/Bienes Nacionales** — Corralco is adjacent to/within Reserva
Nacional Malalcahuello; Farellones-area areas involve fiscal concessions; Chapa Verde is
associated with CODELCO's El Teniente division; Ski Arpa operates as a small private
cat-skiing operation near Los Andes. **The client's requirement of freehold *to the summit*
is therefore genuinely unusual in Chile and is the key screening filter** — most named ski
terrain is precisely *not* available in fee simple. Verify every one of these.

**12.4 Argentina (orientation only).** Ley 26.737 (2011) capped foreign ownership of rural
land at 15% nationally and per province/departamento, with no more than 30% of that share
held by any one nationality, plus a per-holder ceiling expressed as an equivalence to
1,000 ha of Buenos Aires "zona núcleo", and a prohibition on foreign ownership of land
containing or fronting significant permanent water bodies. Decreto 820/2016 relaxed
administration. **I have a recollection that DNU 70/2023 (Milei, Dec 2023) purported to
repeal Ley 26.737, and that this was litigated — I am not confident and the August 2026
news item above suggests the matter is unresolved.** Separately, Decreto-Ley 15.385/44
creates a *Zona de Seguridad de Fronteras* (I recall ~150 km from land frontiers) in which
foreign acquisition requires prior State conformity. **If that is current, it covers the
entire Argentine Andes and is by itself close to disqualifying for this client** — which is
the single most important thing to verify about Argentina.
