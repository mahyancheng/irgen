## 1. THE HEADLINE FINDING

**The mandate as written is structurally hard in the United States, and the reason is a boundary, not a price.**

Nearly every large "mountain ranch" in the Rockies stops at the National Forest line — because that is exactly where the homestead / patent process stopped in 1900. The deed runs up the flank; the summit is federal. Of ~50 properties surveyed, **two** unambiguously carried genuine alpine terrain in fee:

| Property | Deeded acres | Elevation on deed | Ask | USD/deeded ac | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cielo Vista Ranch**, Costilla Co. CO | 83,368 | to **14,047 ft** (Culebra Peak) + 18 peaks >13,000 ft, 22 mi of Sangre de Cristo ridgeline `[P2]` | USD 105,000,000 (2017) | **1,259** | **SOLD 2017** |
| **Tolland Ranch**, Gilpin/Boulder Co. CO | 3,334 | 8,000 ft to **~13,000 ft** (Buckeye & Tennessee Mtns) `[P3]` | USD 9,900,000 | **2,969** | **SOLD** to Conservation Fund → Colorado Parks & Wildlife |

Both **cleared the client's screen easily** — and both are gone. Tolland is now public land. Note what they cost: **USD 1,259–2,969 per deeded acre (MYR 5,084–11,986/acre)** — *cheaper per acre than mid-elevation grazing ranch ground*, which is precisely the arbitrage the client's thesis predicts. The thesis is **economically sound**. The problem is **supply**: this asset class surfaces roughly once every 5–8 years, and when it does, state wildlife agencies, land trusts and trophy buyers are the underbidders.

**Nothing currently listed clears the client's own elevation test.** The best live candidate reaches ~9,500 ft / 2,896 m — under the 3,000 m CO/UT bar, and its state (Utah) is not one where 2,600 m suffices. Two live listings clear the *vertical* test (600 m+) but not the *altitude* test. That is a finding, and it is stated as one.

## 4. PER-PROPERTY NOTES — THE FIVE WORTH PURSUING

### 4.1 Black Hawk Ranch — UT, Carbon County (Helper) — USD 35,000,000 / MYR 141,277,150
20,000 deeded acres at **USD 1,750/ac (MYR 7,064/ac)**. `[P3]` elevation **6,000 → 9,500+ ft**: at **3,500 ft (1,067 m)** this is the **largest verified deeded vertical on any live listing in this study**, and it is the cheapest per acre of any property with real relief. West half is Wasatch Plateau — the Skyline Drive country, which is rolling, high, forested, and famously **low-angle** (see §8: this is exactly the safe-snow terrain profile the mandate needs). East half is Book Cliffs desert and is skiing-irrelevant but is the grazing and hunting asset.
Water: 1.3 mi Price River, 2.0 mi Willow Creek, 0.3 mi Beaver Creek, dozens of springs `[P3]`.
Access: Helper is on US-6 and the Union Pacific; SLC International ~2 hr.
**Unverified:** deeded vs. permit split of the "thousands of acres of surrounding public lands" it "controls access to"; whether the 9,500-ft ground is deeded or is the adjoining Manti-La Sal NF; mineral estate; existing easements. **Highest priority for a title/plat check.**

### 4.2 Royal Elk Ranch — CO, Eagle/Garfield County — USD 15,000,000 / MYR 60,547,350
2,017 deeded at **USD 7,437/ac (MYR 30,019/ac)**. **The only live listing that already has ski runs cut.** `[P3]`: runs "begin on ridge tops and descend through aspen and oak-covered slopes"; aspen groves, spruce and Douglas-fir, meadows, open ridge tops; borders White River National Forest and BLM; **unencumbered by conservation easements**; off-grid cabin + shop/toy barn + yurt. Access via Coffee Pot Road from Dotsero (I-70), ranch entrance 6.5 mi up; **35 minutes to Eagle County Regional Airport (EGE)** — a commercial jet field, which is genuinely unusual for a property this remote.
**Verdict:** at 2,017 acres it is at the very bottom of the client's size band, and USD 7,437/ac is trophy pricing driven by Vail/Aspen proximity rather than by land productivity. But it is the **only live property where the ski product exists today** and the operating thesis could be tested in season one.
**Unverified:** elevation range, number/length/aspect of runs, snow reliability at ~9,000 ft on a south-facing Colorado slope (oak brush in the description is a *warning* — Gambel oak grows on warm, dry, low-snow aspects).

### 4.3 Middle Creek Ranch — CO, Routt County (Oak Creek) — USD 65,000,000 / MYR 262,371,850
9,758 deeded + 995 BLM allotment at **USD 6,661/ac (MYR 26,888/ac)**. Marketed as "the largest available offering in close proximity to world-class skiing in Colorado" `[P3]`. Northern flank of the **Flat Tops** — a range the client's brief already identifies as the right terrain type. `[P3]` **no conservation easements, no public access into or through the deeded lands**, held by one family since 1991, operating **bison and cattle** ranch (so agricultural classification is live and defensible; see §7). 17 paved miles to Steamboat Springs; Yampa Valley Regional (HDN) is close.
**The problem is price.** USD 65 M is 2.6× the price of Black Hawk for half the acreage, and **no elevation range is published anywhere I could reach.** Oak Creek sits at 7,438 ft `[P1]`; the Flat Tops plateau at 10,000–12,000 ft is largely Routt/White River NF and the Flat Tops Wilderness. The likelihood that 9,758 deeded acres includes plateau-top ground is real but **unproven**.
**Action:** this is the single property where a Routt County parcel-map and USGS overlay would most change the answer.

### 4.4 Bighorn Mountain Ranch — WY, Big Horn County (Greybull/Shell) — USD 16,995,000 / MYR 68,600,148
6,713 acres at **USD 2,532/ac (MYR 10,219/ac)** — the best price-to-scale ratio among properties with plausible relief. `[P3]` "nested above the Shell Valley, sprawled across the western edge of the Big Horn Mountain Range"; "the deeded land protects and controls most access to all of approximately 30,000 acres" of surrounding BLM and National Forest — a **strategic access position**, which for a private ski operation is worth more than it looks: it means no public traverse of the ski terrain. Cabins and lodges already built (lodging revenue line). 80 mi to Sheridan, 80 mi to Cody.
**Concerns:** the Bighorns' west face above Shell is dry, and the listing does not state deeded acres separately from total. The Bighorns are, however, one of the **snowiest and most low-angle ranges in Wyoming** — the plateau above Shell Falls holds deep snow well into May.
**Unverified:** deeded/leased split, elevation, whether the 6,713 is all fee.

### 4.5 Carter Creek Ranch — MT, Beaverhead County (Dillon) — USD 15,000,000 / MYR 60,547,350
8,519 deeded at **USD 1,761/ac (MYR 7,107/ac)** — the cheapest large deeded block in a mountain county in the study. `[P3]` steep rocky outcroppings → creek bottoms → benchland meadows; sagebrush with spring-fed aspen groves and dense fir/pine. ### 6.3 Can they be assembled into contiguous skiable terrain?
**Realistically, no — not to the scale this mandate needs.** Reasoning:
- Typical claim is a **lode claim: 1,500 ft × 600 ft ≈ 20.66 acres maximum** `[P1] 30 U.S.C. §23`. Most on offer are 4–10 acres, i.e. fragments.
- To assemble even 600 vertical metres of continuous fall-line you would need **dozens** of claims in one basin from **dozens** of separate owners, several of whom hold **undivided fractional interests** (the Belle Creole "¼ undivided interest" above is the canonical trap — you would be buying into a tenancy-in-common with unknown co-tenants and no partition control).
- The gaps between claims are **National Forest**, so a lift line, a road, or even a groomed descent would cross federal ground and require — a **USFS special-use permit**. Which is the precise dependency the client's thesis exists to avoid.
- Historic assemblies (Silverton Mountain, Telluride) took decades and still ended up permit-dependent.

**Verdict: patented claims are a supporting asset, not a foundation.** Their legitimate uses here are (a) a summit or ridge-top parcel to *complete* a ranch that otherwise stops short, and (b) a high base or hut site. Prices are trivially small in context (USD 11k–150k, MYR 44k–605k).

### 6.4 Liabilities — material and non-obvious
- **CERCLA strict, joint and several liability.** `[P2]` CERCLA attaches to *any* person deemed an owner or operator of a facility from which a hazardous substance is released — **strict liability, no fault required, and joint and several**, meaning a buyer can be assessed the whole cost. Buying a patented claim with a waste-rock dump, adit discharge or tailings pile means buying that. The Tenth Circuit has held that "owner" under CERCLA reaches even the United States notwithstanding the Mining Act's patent mechanics `[P2]` — an indication of how broadly the term is read.
- **Silverton/Eureka is inside a Superfund landscape.** The Bonita Peak Mining District (Gold King Mine, 2015 blowout) is the operative context for the Eureka Gulch claims listed above. **Do not acquire a claim in that drainage without a Phase I/II ESA and environmental counsel.**
- **Good Samaritan protection is thin.** `[P2]` Even voluntary "dry site" cleanup can itself trigger liability if it causes a release; legislative fixes are still proposals.
- **Reclamation obligations** may run with the land under state programmes (Colorado DRMS).
- **Access:** many claims are **landlocked by National Forest with no recorded easement** and are marketed as "requires hiking". A claim you cannot legally drive a snowcat to is worthless for this purpose. **Verify a recorded, appurtenant, motorised-use easement — not a permissive historic use.**

## 7. CONSERVATION EASEMENTS AND PROPERTY TAX (Section F)

### 7.1 Mechanism
The owner sells or donates a perpetual restriction on development to a land trust or agency. Value = (unrestricted appraised value) − (restricted appraised value) `[P2]`. It is **perpetual** and **runs with the land**, binding every future owner.

### 7.2 What it pays
- **Montana:** purchased habitat easements have reached about **USD 100/acre (MYR 404/ac)** where wildlife-habitat value is high and development pressure is real; **significantly less** where it is not `[P2] MSU Extension`. On 10,000 acres that is ~USD 1 M (MYR 4.04 M) — **useful, not transformative.**
- **Colorado:** the mechanism is a **state income tax credit at 90% of the appraised easement value, capped at USD 5,000,000 per donation** for easements donated 2021–2026 `[P1] Colorado DOR / Division of Conservation`. **Critically, the credit certificate is transferable and can be sold to other Colorado taxpayers for cash**, used by the donor over 20 years, or partially refunded by the state in surplus years `[P1]`.

### 7.3 **The foreign-owner answer — and it is better than expected**
- **Federal deduction:** `[P1]` IRC §873(a) limits a nonresident alien's deductions to those connected with income effectively connected to a US trade or business. **§873(b)(2) is an explicit exception: "the deduction for charitable contributions and gifts allowed by section 170" is allowed whether or not connected with ECI.** So a nonresident alien is **not categorically barred** from a §170(h) qualified conservation contribution.
- **But it is close to worthless in practice.** `[P2]` A nonresident alien or foreign corporation generally cannot use a US charitable deduction unless they are already filing on a **net** basis on income effectively connected with a US trade or business — and the deduction only shelters *that* income. A Malaysian buyer holding a ranch that runs cattle would have ECI from the ranching operation, so a deduction has *something* to offset — but that income is small, so the deduction's value is small. **A large easement donation would generate a deduction the client cannot absorb.**
- **The Colorado route sidesteps this entirely.** The Colorado credit is a **state** credit, and it is **transferable for cash**. Its value does not depend on the owner having Colorado (or any US) tax liability — it can be sold. **This is the single strongest reason for a foreign buyer to prefer Colorado over Montana, Wyoming, Idaho or Utah for this mandate.** Cap: USD 5,000,000 (MYR 20,182,450) per donation.
- ⚠️ **Not verified:** whether Colorado's programme imposes any residency or entity-type condition on the *donor* (as opposed to the transferee). This requires Colorado tax counsel before it is relied on. Also unverified: Malaysia–US treaty interaction, and FIRPTA consequences on eventual exit.

### 7.4 **Would an easement forbid a future ski operation?**
**It depends entirely on the drafting, and this is negotiable — but only once.** `[P2]` Easements customarily enumerate **reserved rights**, and land trusts do routinely reserve recreational uses; source material explicitly contemplates "hunting, fishing, hiking **or skiing** in specified areas" as permitted uses, and reserves "those commercial activities that are not in conflict with the conservation values."
**Practical guidance for the client:**
1. **Never buy an already-encumbered property without reading the recorded easement in full.** Telluride Mesa Ranch is already largely encumbered — only ~40 of its eastern 700 acres are outside the existing easement `[P3]` — and its restrictions were written by and for a prior owner.
2. If donating, **negotiate reserved rights up front and in writing**: motorised over-snow travel on identified routes; grooming; cutting and maintenance of designated ski corridors; a stated number of guest beds; commercial guiding. Land trusts commonly resist **new road construction**, **vegetation removal at scale** (i.e. cutting runs), and **commercial recreation with public access** — the three things a ski operation most needs.
3. An easement permanently caps the property's resale value. It converts an appreciating asset into a yielding one. That is consistent with the client's "keep costs low" thesis but **inconsistent with any later "expand if demand justifies it"** step. **These two parts of the mandate are in tension.**
4. **Middle Creek and Royal Elk are both marketed as unencumbered** `[P3]` — meaning the easement value has *not* yet been harvested and is available to a buyer. That is real, quantifiable option value in the Colorado properties specifically.

### 7.5 State agricultural property-tax classification `[P1]`
Use-based; **lost if the qualifying use stops.**

| State | Threshold | Income / use test |

### 8.1 Ski / recreation liability insurance
**I could not obtain a bindable quote or a per-operation premium figure.** What I did establish:
- `[P2]` **NSAA 2020-21 Economic Analysis: insurance expense at responding ski areas rose 54% year over year**, reaching ~2% of total revenue.

**`[E]` Assessment.** The risk profile changes sharply across the client's three phases:
1. **Owner and invited guests only, no fee.** Covered as a farm/ranch umbrella exposure. `[E]` low incremental cost — perhaps USD 5,000–20,000/yr (MYR 20,000–81,000) of additional umbrella. **This is the phase the thesis should live in.**
2. **Members / club, fee-paying.** Now a commercial recreation exposure requiring specialty placement. `[E]` USD 50,000–250,000/yr (MYR 202,000–1,009,000) depending on skier-days, terrain angle, and whether snowcats carry passengers.
3. **Any avalanche terrain with paying guests.** This is the cliff edge. Snowcat/guided operations in avalanche terrain are among the hardest recreation risks to place at any price.
**The client's "no lifts" plan reduces one exposure (lift entrapment/evacuation) but the passenger-carrying snowcat and avalanche exposures are the dominant ones and are not reduced at all.** Flag this as **requiring a real broker submission before committing capital** — this is the single largest unpriced item in the model.

### 8.2 Wildfire insurance
- `[P2]` Colorado homeowner insurance **non-renewals rose 77% from 2018 to 2023** and the trend continued through 2025–26; Colorado issued an **emergency moratorium** on non-renewals after the Aspen Acres fire.
- `[P2]` **Colorado launched a FAIR Plan in April 2025 — capped at USD 750,000 (MYR 3,027,368) residential coverage.** For a lodge or a main house this cap is likely **below replacement cost**, leaving a gap.
- `[P2]` High-risk Colorado homes outside the FAIR Plan see specialty (E&S) premiums of **USD 7,000 to 22,000+ per year (MYR 28,255–88,803+)** per structure, depending on replacement cost, slope, vegetation, fire protection, roof and completed mitigation.
- `[P2]` **Montana has no FAIR Plan.** The surplus-lines market is the only backstop. `[E]` This makes Montana *structurally worse* than Colorado for insurability of improvements.
- `[P2]` National E&S premium volume rose 13.2% YoY to USD 46.2 bn through mid-2025 — the market is hardening, not softening. The broader market has been described as "the hardest insurance market in 40 years", driven by wildfire.

**`[E]` Design implication, and it is a favourable one:** the exposure is in **structures**, not in land. A strategy of **few, small, defensible, metal-roofed buildings with wide mitigated perimeters** — precisely the "extremely low CAPEX" posture the client already wants — keeps this line at `[E]` USD 15,000–60,000/yr (MYR 60,500–242,000). A lodge programme would multiply it. **Wildfire insurance argues for the low-CAPEX thesis, not against it.**

### 8.3 Severed mineral estates
- `[P2]` **"Most of the mineral rights in the western U.S. have been partially or entirely severed."** Split estates are "more common across the western United States than most buyers expect", and Colorado and New Mexico are named among the states where severance is very common.
- Where the mineral estate is severed, it is generally **dominant**: the mineral owner may access the surface to develop, and the surface owner cannot prevent it. For a private ski mountain, a third party's right to put a drill pad or an access road across the terrain is a **material and uninsurable-against-use risk**, not a technicality.
- **How to check `[P2]`:** (1) full title search back to patent — not a title *commitment*, a full chain; (2) the **title insurance policy must state expressly** whether minerals are included; (3) obtain **written proof of mineral ownership from the seller**; (4) search the county recorder for reservations in prior deeds — severance appears as a specific reservation or conveyance of subsurface rights; (5) check state oil & gas commission and BLM records for existing leases; (6) where minerals are severed and held by identifiable parties, **price the cost of buying them back into the acquisition budget.**
- **Note:** on a **patented mining claim** the patent conveyed the locatable minerals *with* the surface `[P2]` — so patented claims are, ironically, more likely than ranch ground to have an intact unified estate. That is a genuine point in their favour.

## 9. SNOW AND AVALANCHE (Section H)

### 9.1 The physics of the problem `[P2]`
- The **continental** snow climate — most of **Colorado, Wyoming and New Mexico** — has less snowfall, colder temperatures, lower-density snow and, because the snowpack is thin, a **high temperature gradient**. That gradient drives **faceting and depth hoar**.
- Colorado is described as **notorious** for depth hoar: "by February, the entire snowpack may consist of weak faceted snow."
- The **intermountain** climate — the **Wasatch, most of Idaho, most of Montana**, the Blue Mountains, and parts of southwestern Colorado — gets more snow, so **depth hoar is typically an early-season phenomenon** that gets buried and heals rather than persisting all winter.
- Continental snowpacks produce **deep persistent slab** avalanches: low-frequency, very high-consequence, and **hard to forecast for weeks or months**. Overall avalanche danger is highest in continental zones.
- Maritime snowpacks are least susceptible; none of the client's target states are maritime.

### 9.2 Direct consequence for the mandate
**This inverts part of the client's geographic instinct.** The brief lists Colorado ranges (Grand Mesa, Flat Tops, Rabbit Ears, Park Range) as the safer choice because they are flatter than the San Juans. That is right on **terrain** but wrong on **snowpack climate**: Colorado is the worst continental snowpack in the study *everywhere in the state*, flat ranges included. Flat terrain in a continental snowpack is safe because it is flat, not because the snowpack is good.

Conversely **Utah's Wasatch Plateau and most of Idaho and Montana sit in the intermountain climate**, where the persistent-weak-layer problem is materially less severe and more seasonal.

### 9.3 Ranked recommendation `[E]`, built on the `[P2]` climate classification

**Tier 1 — intermountain snowpack + inherently low-angle terrain:**
1. **Wasatch Plateau, Utah (Skyline Drive country)** — intermountain snowpack, rolling plateau at 9,000–11,000 ft, deep snow, low angles, abundant north aspects. **Black Hawk Ranch sits on its northern convergence.** This is the single best snow-safety/terrain combination found in the study, and it is also the cheapest per acre.
2. **Bighorn Mountains, Wyoming** — a broad high plateau rather than a knife-edge range; long low-angle spurs; deep late-lying snow. Technically continental, but the terrain does the safety work. **Bighorn Mountain Ranch sits on its western edge.**

**Tier 2 — intermountain, but access-constrained:**
3. **Pioneer / Beaverhead / Big Hole ranges, SW Montana** `[P2]`: the Beaverhead-Deerlodge is "3 million acres... wild and remote... best suited for **spring touring or snowmobile access**." Read that as: **midwinter road access is the binding constraint, not snow.** The Idaho Pioneers near Sun Valley offer "steep couloirs to **low-angle glades**." Snow quality is excellent (cold, dry, high-latitude). Carter Creek, Nicholia Creek and Centennial Ranch are all in this pocket, and it is the cheapest land in the study.
4. **Sawtooth / Smoky / Boulder, Idaho** `[P2]`: snowpack "varies each year"; after **mid-February** there is good snow with a decent base; **early April** offers "a snowpack still deep but more stable, with less avalanche hazard than in early or mid-winter." **This is a spring-season asset, not a January asset.**

**Tier 3 — good terrain, worst snowpack:**
5. **Grand Mesa / Flat Tops / Park Range, Colorado** — genuinely excellent low-angle high terrain (Grand Mesa is a 10,000-ft flat-topped plateau; the Flat Tops are the same), and Steamboat-area orographics deliver real snow depth. But it is **continental snowpack with deep persistent slabs**, so the operational protocol must be **hard-coded low-angle only** — under 30°, no terrain traps, no runouts under steep starting zones. **Middle Creek and Royal Elk are both here.** Manageable, but it requires professional avalanche staffing every season, which is an OPEX line the thesis has not yet budgeted.

**Avoid for this mandate:** the San Juans (steep, corniced, continental, and Superfund-adjacent) and the Lolo/Bitterroot low country (Lolo Ranch tops out near 6,000 ft — **too low for reliable snow in a warming climate**, and this is the reason to pass on it despite its ready-made runs).
