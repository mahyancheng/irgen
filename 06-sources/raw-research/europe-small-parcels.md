# Europe Reopened at 40 Hectares — Small Private Ski-Mountain Parcels
### Screening report for a Malaysia-based private buyer (non-EU, non-resident, reports in MYR)

**Brief:** ~40 ha, ~300 m vertical, benchmarked on 吉雪滑雪场 (Jixue, Jilin City): 400,000 m², 13 runs, longest run 910 m at 19° mean, slope range 6–33°. Private use only. Registered title **must** include the skiable slope **and its top**. Minimal CAPEX/OPEX. Phase 1 = snowcat / snowmobile / touring / simple rope tow; **no lift required**. No existing lift, hotel, power or water needed.

**Date of research:** 28 August 2026. All prices are as-published on the dates noted; listings move.

## 1. Headline conclusion

Reopening Europe at 40 ha is correct in principle — the cadastre/assembly objection genuinely does weaken. But **the binding constraint moves from cadastre to nationality.** For a Malaysian individual buying directly, most of the European alpine arc is shut or heavily conditioned, and the two things that make Europe attractive (cleared pistes, existing road and power) sit disproportionately in the countries that are shut.

The screen resolves to a short list:

- **Open, nationality-blind, no permit tied to the passport: Sweden, Norway, France, Portugal, Czechia.**
- **Open but with a real permit gate on rural land for non-EU buyers: Spain** (Ministry of Defence consent in designated zones, incl. the Pyrenees), **Greece** (border prefectures only).
- **Effectively closed to a direct individual purchase, company route only: Romania, Bulgaria, Serbia, Bosnia, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Albania, Kosovo.**
- **Closed or near-closed for this asset class: Switzerland, Austria, Slovakia, Poland, Slovenia (friction), Italy (see §2.3 — the reciprocity test probably fails for *agricultural* land).**

The single most under-considered answer is **Scandinavia**, which the brief did not list: it is the only part of Europe that is simultaneously (a) legally indifferent to a Malaysian passport, (b) snow-reliable on a 30-year view at elevations the client can actually afford, and (c) full of small, failing, cheap ski areas. See §5.

### 2.2 The structural problem with the SPV route

Romania, Bulgaria, Serbia and the rest of the Balkans are reachable only through a locally incorporated company. That collides directly with the client's two hardest constraints:

1. **"The registered title must include the skiable slope and the top of it."** Through an SPV the *company* is on the title, not the buyer. Whether that satisfies the client is a question for them, not for me — but it should be put to them explicitly, because it converts a freehold into a shareholding, with a local registered office, local director duties, annual filings, statutory audit thresholds and a corporate tax return. [E]
2. **"Extremely low OPEX."** A dormant SPV in Romania or Bulgaria is not free — accounting, filings and a registered address are a permanent annual cost against an asset that generates nothing. [E]

An SPV is defensible when the prize is large. For a 40 ha private toy it is a poor trade, and it is the reason I would not restart the Balkans even now that scale is no longer the objection.

### 2.3 Italy — why reciprocity probably fails, and what survives

Italy is the country with the best supply for this mandate (see §4.1) and it is the one I cannot clear.

- Article 16 of the preliminary provisions to the Civil Code: a foreigner enjoys civil rights **on condition of reciprocity**. For a non-EU citizen who is **not regularly resident** in Italy, the notary must verify reciprocity before the deed, using the MAECI/Farnesina country tables [P2]. EU/EEA nationals, and non-EU nationals holding the right residence permit, are exempt.
- Crucially, **reciprocity is tested right by right, not country by country.** The published practice for non-resident buyers is framed around *secondary residences and holiday homes* with surface caps (commonly cited: ≤100 m² for apartment-hotel units; ≤1,000 m² for a villa/individual unit) [P2]. Nothing in that framing addresses a 40–200 ha agricultural holding.
- **Malaysia's own law is the mirror the Italian notary looks into.** Malaysia's general rule is that **foreigners cannot purchase agricultural-category land**; state authority consent is required for every foreign purchase; and the narrow agricultural exceptions (Federal Territories, Sabah, Negeri Sembilan) require **RM 1,000,000 minimum, ≥5 acres, and commercial-scale agricultural or agro-tourism use** [P2].
- **[E] Conclusion:** an Italian could not, as a private individual, buy a 40 ha hill pasture in Malaysia for private recreation. A notary applying the reciprocity test symmetrically should therefore refuse to close a Malaysian individual's purchase of an Italian *malga* or *alpeggio*. I rate this **likely to fail, not certain** — it is a notary-level judgement and worth one paid opinion before writing Italy off, because Italy has the best inventory.
- **What survives [E]:** (i) buy through a company. Reciprocity also governs company participation, but Malaysia is permissive on foreign shareholding, so corporate reciprocity is more likely satisfied than individual reciprocity for farmland; (ii) better, buy through a company incorporated **in another EU member state**, which then acquires in Italy under freedom of establishment and never meets the reciprocity test at all; (iii) obtain Italian residence, which removes the test entirely. All three are lawyer-shaped, not buyer-shaped.

### 2.4 France — SAFER is the gate, not the passport

France imposes **no nationality restriction** on land ownership. The exposure is procedural and it is real:

- SAFER holds a **right of information and a right of pre-emption** over agricultural land, forests and certain rural property. The notary must notify; SAFER may **substitute itself for the intended buyer** to preserve agricultural or environmental use [P1/P2].
- SAFER may also pre-empt **with a revision of price** — i.e. it can take the property at a price it considers the true agricultural value, which is a distinct mechanism from a simple veto [P2].
- **[E] Practical reading:** SAFER does not target foreigners as such, but a non-resident foreign buyer acquiring an *alpage* for private recreational use presents almost exactly the profile SAFER exists to displace — non-agricultural use, no installation of a farmer, price disconnected from agricultural value. The mitigation used in practice is to buy with a **lease to a working farmer already in place** (a *bail rural* or a pastoral convention), which preserves the agricultural use SAFER is protecting, and to pre-brief the local SAFER before signing. Budget for it.
- Note the transaction that proves the price level: the **Département de Haute-Savoie bought a 64 ha alpage with a 340 m² chalet at La Chapelle-d'Abondance for €700,000** — found on Leboncoin [P2]. Consequential in two ways: it is a real, closed, French alpage comparable at **~€10,900/ha**, and it shows public bodies competing for exactly this asset class.

### 2.5 Spain — the most workable of the southern options

- **No nationality bar on rural land ownership.** The gate is **Ley 8/1975** military consent for non-EU buyers of **rustic** property in designated zones [P2].
- **Where it bites:** islands, Strait of Gibraltar, **French and Portuguese borderlands**, Cartagena, **Galicia**, Bay of Cádiz. The **Pyrenees are inside the French borderland**, so any Pyrenean purchase carries the permit.
- **Where it does not bite:** the **Cantabrian range in Asturias and León**, the **Sistema Central**, and **Sierra Nevada** are not in the listed restricted areas [E, from the list as published]. That is the part of Spain to work.
- Consent is a documented administrative step with a stated 2–4 month response, commonly longer; the notary and registry will not proceed without it [P2]. I found **no evidence** that it is refused as a matter of course, and equally **no evidence** that it is "routine" — do not represent it to the client as a formality.

## 3. Snow reality check — the hard screen

The client's own instruction — base above 1,200 m and top above 1,600 m in the Alps and Pyrenees — is close to right but slightly generous at the base. The literature:

- **Abegg's 100-day rule** [P2]: a resort is snow-reliable if in 7 winters out of 10 there is 30–50 cm for ≥100 days between 1 December and 15 April. Abegg's snow-reliability line for **Switzerland was 1,200 m**, rising **~150 m per 1 °C** of warming.
- Historic thresholds: **~1,050 m on the northern rim of the Alps, ~1,500 m in the Southern Alps** [P2].
- **Current consensus is 1,500–1,800 m, higher in some winters** [P2]. **+2 °C moves the reliability line up ~300 m**; at +1 °C only ~75% of European Alpine resorts remain reliable, ~61% at +2 °C, ~30% at +4 °C [P2].
- **Snowmaking cannot rescue a season below ~1,500–1,600 m** at current intensities [P2] — and snowmaking is CAPEX and OPEX the client has explicitly refused, plus it needs the water the client has said they do not want to provide.

**Thresholds I would apply [E], tightened where the brief was generous:**

| Range | Minimum base | Minimum top | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Western/Southern Alps, Pyrenees | **1,500 m** | **1,900 m** | The brief's 1,200/1,600 is a 1990s number. Below 1,500 m base you are buying a 20-year asset, not a 40-year one. |
| Northern rim of the Alps (N. Tyrol, Bavaria, Salzkammergut) | 1,300 m | 1,700 m | Wetter, colder northern flow; ~200 m of slack |
| Apennines | 1,600 m | 2,000 m | Mediterranean, high variance, and worse than latitude suggests |
| Carpathians (RO/SK/PL/UA) | 1,200 m | 1,600 m | Continental and colder than the Alps at the same height; Jasná (900–2,024 m) is rated very snow-sure [P2] |
| Rila / Pirin (BG) | 1,400 m | 1,900 m | Bansko 990–2,530 m (1,540 m diff.), Borovets 1,300–2,540 m [P2] — the working terrain is high |
| Dinarides (BA/ME/RS) | 1,300 m | 1,700 m | Very high precipitation on the coastal-facing flanks; wet, heavy snow |
| Scandinavia (Norway inland, Swedish fjäll) | **500 m** | **800 m** | Latitude substitutes for altitude. This is the whole point of §5. |
| Scandinavia (coastal south — Agder, Rogaland) | Not viable at any accessible height | — | See Krågeland, §5.3 |

**Say it plainly where it applies.** Several of the cheapest assets in Europe are cheap for exactly one reason:

- The **Bierzo village (León) at 20 ha for €420,000** sits at **~700 m** [P2]. There is no ski mountain there. It is a farm.
- **Les Fourgs (Jura, France)**, asking **€800,000**, runs roughly **900–1,150 m**. It is a nordic and family day-hill and it is on the wrong side of the snow line. The article itself notes buyers were deterred — though by *land-title problems*, which is its own warning [P2].
- **Krågeland Alpinsenter (Kvinesdal, Agder, Norway)**, asking **NOK 2.5m**, **opened two days in a season** and the owner cited electricity prices [P2]. Coastal southern Norway at 250–400 m is maritime and marginal. Cheap, and worthless for this purpose.
- Romanian listings adjacent to **Rarău** (Câmpulung Moldovenesc) at **€30,000 for 1,359 m² and €75,000 for 3,200 m²** [P3] price at **€22–23 per m² = €220,000+/ha**. That is resort-adjacent building-plot pricing, not mountain-land pricing, and it tells you the Romanian market prices proximity to a lift, not vertical.

## 5. Scandinavia — the case the brief did not make

This is the most important finding in the report and it is worth stating separately.

**Why it fits:**

1. **Legally open.** Neither Sweden nor Norway has a nationality-based restriction on land ownership [P2]. Sweden's *jordförvärvslagen* permit and Norway's *konsesjonsloven* concession are **land-use tests applied to everybody**, decided by a county board or a municipality on whether the land stays in productive use — not on where the buyer's passport was issued. A private buyer with no intention to farm faces the same question a Swedish buyer would.
2. **Snow, without altitude.** The Alpine problem is that snow reliability is climbing through 1,500–1,800 m and land at that height is either expensive, communal, or inside a park. In inland Norway and the Swedish fjäll the reliable season sits at **500–900 m**, where land is cheap and privately held. Bjorli delivers **650 m of vertical** with a base around 600 m [P2].
3. **The right supply.** Small Scandinavian ski areas are failing on exactly the economics the client is not exposed to — lift electricity, staff, insurance, day-ticket demand. Bjorli went under with NOK 40m of debt in 2025 [P2]; Krågeland's owner named **electricity prices** as the reason to quit [P2]. A private owner running a snowcat and a touring skin track has none of those costs.
4. **Treeless tops.** The Swedish *kalfjäll* and Norwegian *snaufjell* above the tree line give open, moderate-angle, already-cleared ground — the *malga*/*alpage* quality the brief was chasing, at a fraction of the Alpine price and with no grazing servitude culture to inherit. [E]

**What to check before recommending it:**

- **Sweden:** whether the target sits in a *glesbygd* zone triggering *förvärvstillstånd*, and what the Länsstyrelsen's disposition is toward a non-farming, non-resident acquirer. Most of Jämtland/Härjedalen is precisely such a zone. This is the one genuine risk. [E]
- **Sweden: *allemansrätten* (right of public access).** I was **unable to verify this within the search budget** and it is therefore stated as **[E], from background knowledge, NOT sourced in this report.** The claim I would test is that the right of public access is constitutionally recognised in Sweden, applies to *utmark*-type land, cannot be contracted out of by a landowner, and would entitle the public to ski and tour across the client's mountain. **If true it is close to a dealbreaker for a "private" ski mountain and it must be the first thing a Swedish lawyer is asked.** Do not present Sweden to the client until this is confirmed or refuted.
- **Norway: *allemannsretten*** is the direct analogue and applies to *utmark*, which is precisely this terrain. Same status: **[E], unverified here, must be checked first.**
- **Norway:** whether the parcel exceeds 100 dekar (10 ha) and thus needs concession — at 40 ha (400 dekar) it will. Concession is granted or refused by the municipality on purpose; a recreational non-resident buyer is not the archetype the statute favours. [E]
- **Reindeer husbandry rights (*renskötselrätt* / *reindriftsrett*).** **[E], unverified here — no source in this report.** My understanding is that these rights burden very large areas of the Swedish and Norwegian fjäll in favour of Sámi communities, survive a sale, and are statutorily protected; in the Swedish fjäll the coverage is extensive. If so, this is the Scandinavian analogue of the Alpine grazing servitude and it is the stronger of the two. **Must be checked parcel by parcel against the *renbetesland* mapping before any offer.**

**Bottom line [E]:** Scandinavia is the only place in Europe where I can evidence that the passport is not a bar *and* the snow is secure at an affordable altitude. That combination exists nowhere else on this list. It is offset by two potential burdens — public access rights and reindeer rights — **which I could not verify before the search budget ran out**, and which do not prevent ownership but do cut against the word "private". **Recommendation: commission one Swedish and one Norwegian rural-property opinion on those two points before anything else in this report is acted on.** If both come back manageable, Scandinavia is the answer. If public access cannot be excluded, Scandinavia fails the brief on "private" and the mandate falls back to France and Spain.

## 6. Risks that appear specifically at 40 ha

At 400 hectares these are nuisances. At 40 they are existential, because a single defect can sever the parcel from its road or cap its vertical.

### 6.1 Legal road access — the parcel that does not own its way in
A 40 ha alpine parcel very often reaches the public road only across someone else's land.
- **Italy** provides a statutory cure. **Art. 1051 c.c.** grants the owner of a *fondo intercluso* a **compulsory right of way** where there is no exit to the public road, or none obtainable without excessive expense or inconvenience; **art. 1052** extends it to non-enclosed land in defined cases. Compensation proportionate to the damage is payable [P1].
- **But the Cassazione has held that mere physical ability to cross a neighbour's land does not defeat enclosure — a stable legal title, a true real right of passage, is required** [P2]. Read the other way: **if your access rests on nothing but the neighbour's tolerance, you do not have access.** The remedy is a lawsuit.
- Enclosure is assessed on **the property as a whole**, with an exception where part of it is genuinely inaccessible [P2]. On a long, thin ski parcel that exception matters: the road may serve the base and not the top.
- **Action:** demand the *estratto di mappa* / cadastral extract and the registered servitudes before offer, in every jurisdiction. Never accept "there's a track."

### 6.2 Winter clearing
No European jurisdiction obliges a public authority to plough a private mountain track to a private parcel. Where an existing closed ski area is bought, the road was historically cleared **because a commercial operator paid for it or the commune did it for the resort**; on closure that stops. Les Fourgs, San Simone and Campitello are all cases where the ploughing rationale disappears with the business. **[E] Assume the client owns the ploughing cost from the public road up, and price it as the largest single recurring OPEX line** — which sits badly against "extremely low OPEX".

### 6.3 What is above the parcel — the vertical cap
"Title must include the top" is the hardest clause in the brief, because in most of Europe **the top is not privately owned.**
- **Italy:** high ground is very frequently burdened by **usi civici** and **domini collettivi**. **Law 168/2017** confirms collective assets as **inalienable, indivisible, imprescriptible and non-usucapible**, with a perpetual agro-silvo-pastoral destination, assimilated in regime to state property [P1]. **A sale of land in *demanio civico* is void.** Article 3's enumeration is *tassativa* — not to be read by analogy [P2]. **This is the single largest hidden risk in buying an Italian malga**, and the standard diligence is a *certificato di destinazione urbanistica* plus a specific *verifica demaniale/usi civici* from the region.
- **Spain:** high ground is dominated by **montes de utilidad pública** and *comunales*, which are not on the market. [E]
- **France:** above the *alpage* the ground is commonly communal or *sectional*. [E]
- **Romania/Balkans:** the summit is usually state forest or park.
- **Action:** the parcel's **upper boundary** must be established on the cadastral map *before* valuing the vertical. A 40 ha parcel whose title stops 80 m below the ridge does not meet the brief, and no amount of price makes it meet the brief.

### 6.4 Grazing and commonage that survive the sale
- **Austria/Alps:** *Servituten* — including **Weiderechte** and stock-driving *Wegerechte* — **survive a change of owner on both sides**. Registration in the *Grundbuch* gives third-party effect, **but "offenkundige Dienstbarkeiten" (apparent servitudes) bind third parties even unregistered**, arising by prescription after **30 years** [P2]. **So a clean Grundbuch extract is not proof the land is unburdened.** Walk it, and ask who has been driving stock across it.
- **Romania:** *obști* and *composesorate* — associative commons — were restituted under Law 18/1991, Law 1/2000 and Law 247/2005, with a single title issued to the association and **all restitution caps abandoned by Law 247/2005** [P1/P2]. Restituted surface was benchmarked to the **1921 agrarian reform**. Grazing rights attached to these bodies are collective and long-lived.
- **Italy:** see §6.3 — *usi civici* are the same problem in a stronger form, because they can void the sale rather than merely burden it.
- **Scandinavia:** reindeer husbandry rights and *allemansrätten*/*allemannsretten* — see §5.

### 6.5 Restitution
Live in Romania (the Law 247/2005 régime is expansive and litigation-generating), and in the Western Balkans generally. Not a live issue in France, Spain, Italy, Norway or Sweden. **[E] This risk is now a reason to prefer Western Europe, not a reason to buy scale in the East.**

### 6.6 Inherited lift liabilities — a hazard unique to buying a closed ski area
If the client buys a dormant resort rather than bare land, they inherit the lifts.
- Italian accounting and regulatory practice requires operators, at end of use, to **remove surface and buried installations and restore the ground**, with a provision (*fondo di smantellamento e ripristino*, OIC 31) recognised against the asset when the obligation is assumed [P2].
- Italy has a **Ministero del Turismo fund for the modernisation, safety and *dismissione* of ski lifts and snowmaking** [P1] — i.e. there is public money for removal, which partly de-risks it.
- **[E] Read-across:** the client wants no lift. Buying San Simone or Campitello means acquiring chairlifts and drag-lifts that are a **removal liability**, not an asset — and Campitello's five lots are 71% lift value by the auction base. **Prefer bare land, or negotiate that the vendor removes the lifts pre-completion.**

## 7. Where the mandate actually points

1. **Sweden or inland Norway** — legally open, snow-secure, cheap, correct asset shape. Cost: a nationality-blind but **discretionary** permit (SE) / concession (NO), plus two burdens I could not verify — public access rights and reindeer rights. **Ranked first on evidence, but conditional on those two checks.**
2. **France, an *alpage* above 1,500 m** — legally open, proven comparable (La Chapelle-d'Abondance, 64 ha, €700k), but SAFER must be managed and public bodies compete.
3. **Spain, Cantabrian range (Asturias/León), outside the military-consent zone** — €9,742/ha average agricultural value means 40 ha lands near **MYR 1.8m** before altitude premium, and no permit if the parcel avoids Galicia and the Pyrenees.
4. **Italy** — best inventory in Europe, San Simone is uncannily close to the Jixue benchmark, and the country is probably shut to a Malaysian individual for agricultural land. **Buy one notarial opinion before abandoning it.**
5. **Everything else** — Austria, Switzerland, Slovakia, Poland closed; the Balkans and Romania/Bulgaria are SPV projects that defeat both "title in my name" and "extremely low OPEX".
