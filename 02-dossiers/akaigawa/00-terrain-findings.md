## The headline

**Akaigawa is a volcanic caldera 6–7 km across with its farmland on the floor and a continuous rim
above it, and it sits outside every national and quasi-national park in Hokkaido.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Caldera floor** | ~150 m; village office at **138.28 m** `[P3]` |
| **Rim high point** | 大黒山 Daikoku-yama, **725 m** `[P3]` |
| **Relief** | **~575 m — any 300 m sub-section selectable** |
| **Park status** | **Outside every park** `[P1]` — see below |
| **Snow** | Described as 北海道でも有数の豪雪地帯 `[P3]`. **Akaigawa's own AMeDAS normals: unconfirmed.** Kiroro (summit 1,180 m) operates in the same village. |
| **Volcanic hazard** | Extinct, 1.7–1.3 Ma → **no hazard zoning** `[P2]` |
| **Access** | 国道393 (Maple Road) from Otaru; 道道36 to Yoichi/Niki |
| **Aspect** | Inner wall faces inward on all sides → **north- and north-east-facing inner slopes on the south and west rim** |

## Why this village and not its neighbours

This is the load-bearing finding, and it is `[P1]`:

> **Akaigawa is absent from the 11-municipality list of the 
> ニセコ積丹小樽海岸国定公園 and absent from the 支笏洞爺国立公園 list.** Its neighbours Yoichi,
> Kutchan and Kyōgoku are inside one or the other. **Akaigawa and Niki sit in the gap between
> them.**

That gap is the whole opportunity. Park designation is what makes snowcats and snowmobiles a
criminal matter (自然公園法 車馬等乗入れ規制, 1 yr / ¥1 m) **even on private land inside the
boundary**. Akaigawa is the rare place with serious snow, serious relief, and no park overlay.

The geology explains the shape: a caldera puts flat, farmable ground on the floor and steep,
unfarmable ground on the rim, **in a single continuous landform, with the boundary between them at
a predictable elevation.** That is precisely the 1897-grant pattern this study predicted — and a
hiking record has the climb from the floor starting at ~150 m and **steepening from ~250 m**, i.e.
the productive band begins exactly where the farmland stops.

## The finding that reverses the acquisition problem `[P1]`

This overturns the framing that has run through the entire study:

| Land category | What buying it requires |
|---|---|
| **山林 (forest)** | **No prior permission.** A notification to the mayor within **90 days** after the fact (森林法). From April 2026 the form records the buyer's nationality. Over 1 ha adds a 国土利用計画法 filing. |
| **農地 (farmland)** | **農業委員会 permission is mandatory** (農地法第3条). An unpermitted transaction is **legally void** — not penalised, void. |

> **The hill is the easy part to buy. The farm is the hard part.** Every previous version of this
> study assumed the reverse — that the ranch would be straightforward and the slope would be locked
> up in 国有林.

**Strategic consequence:** build the acquisition around the **forest parcel**, and treat any
farmland as optional. The 農地法第3条 常時従事要件 (~150 days/yr of farming) that dominated the
Japan analysis is **avoided entirely** by not buying 農地. If a base area is needed, target 宅地 or
原野 rather than 農地.

Tenure statistics confirm the terrain thesis `[P1]`: of Hokkaido's 5.54 M ha of forest, national
forest is **55.3%** — but it is 奥地の脊梁山地や水源地域に広く分布, *remote spine ranges and
headwater areas*. Private forest is **27.9%**, prefectural 11.0%, municipal 5.8%. **About 45% is
non-national, and it sits on exactly the low, road-accessible, farm-adjacent hills this mandate
wants.** The earlier reading — that Hokkaido's 55% national forest made it *worse* than the rest of
Japan — was measuring the wrong thing. What matters is not the share, but where it lies.

## The gradient question, unresolved

The workstream computed 300 m over a 910 m run = **18.2°** for the inner caldera wall, and offered
it as a match to 吉雪's 19° average. **Against the revised specification in
`07-terrain-and-snow/06-gradient-spec.md` (22–28° target) that is too flat.**

It is probably not fatal, because **575 m of relief means a 300 m band can be selected rather than
taken as the average** — and the hiking account has the wall steepening above 250 m. The steeper
upper band is likely to sit in the target range while the 18.2° figure describes the whole profile
including the gentle apron.

**This is now the first thing to measure, not the last.** It decides whether Akaigawa delivers
336 m of vertical from a compact parcel or 218 m from a sprawling one.

## Second-choice, worth a parallel track: 夕張 Yubari

**冷水山 Reisui-zan (Mt. Racey) — 404 m of *measured* vertical from a 298 m base, 18 cut runs,
longest 3,500 m.** `[P2]`

The distinguishing feature is that the terrain is already developed and the ownership is distressed:
夕張リゾート went bankrupt in February 2021, after the city sold to a Chinese buyer for ¥240 M in
2017 and the asset passed to a Hong Kong fund. **This is the fastest route from "terrain identified"
to "terrain owned"** — and the only candidate where the vertical is measured rather than inferred.

Against it: buying a former commercial area means buying its liabilities — lifts, buildings,
demolition exposure — which is the Grandeco problem this study documented. Worth a look precisely
because it is the opposite risk profile to Akaigawa.

## Two corrections to the earlier brief `[P1]`

1. **Hidaka is now a national park.** 日高山脈襟裳国定公園 was abolished and re-designated
   **日高山脈襟裳十勝国立公園 on 25 June 2024** — 252,177 ha, taking in 日高町, 平取町 and 新冠町.
   **Hokkaido now has 7 national parks and 4 quasi-national parks, not 6 + 5.** The earlier
   instruction to work the 日高西麓 is withdrawn.
2. **京極町 and 喜茂別町 are both inside 支笏洞爺国立公園.** Working their west side does not help —
   羊蹄山 is both park and 道有林. **This kills the 京極町字春日 93.7 ha / ¥60,000,000 lead** that
   was carried as a live candidate.

Conversely, **沼田町 and 秩父別町 are NOT in the 暑寒別天売焼尻国定公園** — that caution was
unnecessary. But Numata's own hills are low (its municipal ski area has 80 m of vertical).
