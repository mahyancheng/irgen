# Country brief — New Zealand

**Verdict: the client's preferred jurisdiction is, on the evidence, the hardest one in the
study — and it fails for structural reasons that no amount of searching will fix.**

Three findings govern everything. Each is independently sufficient to make New Zealand
difficult; together they make the stated thesis close to unexecutable in its pure form.

---

## Finding 1 — Tenure review gave the alpine tops to the Crown, and the door is now shut

From 1998 to 2022, tenure review under the Crown Pastoral Land Act 1998 systematically
**freeholded the lower productive country and transferred the high-altitude tops to the Crown
as conservation land.** This is the exact inverse of what the mandate requires.

| Station | Freeholded to the lessee | Transferred to the Crown |
|---|---|---|
| Earnscleugh (Old Man Range) | ~16,600 ha | **8,060 ha, "mostly the higher altitude land"** `[P2]` |
| Godley Peaks | 2,676 ha (376 ha covenanted) | **11,875 ha** `[P1]` |
| Hukarere Station | 5,529 ha (394 ha covenanted) | 1,648 ha `[P1]` |
| Dunstan Downs | — | **99 % of the lease** `[P1]` |

The **Crown Pastoral Land Reform Act 2022 ended tenure review** on 18 May 2022. `[P2]`

> **The consequence is decisive: there is no longer any statutory pathway to convert a
> pastoral lease over alpine tops into freehold. What is leasehold today stays leasehold,
> indefinitely.** Any New Zealand acquisition must be screened on the tenure that exists
> now. There is no conversion optionality to underwrite. `[E]`

## Finding 3 — The screening regime is adverse to *this specific* end-use

The Overseas Investment (National Interest Test and Other Matters) Amendment Act 2025
(Royal Assent 19 Dec 2025, in force 6 March 2026) introduced a single national interest test
and a fast track — **but residential land, farmland and fishing quota are expressly carved
out** and remain under the older, slower, harder screening. `[P2]`

For a Malaysian buyer of a high-country station:

- The land is "sensitive" on several grounds simultaneously — non-urban over 5 ha, adjoining
  conservation land, adjoining lakebed, adjoining reserve. **Consent is required regardless
  of price.** `[E]`
- Consent requires the **investor test** *and* the **benefit to New Zealand test** — seven
  statutory factors, requiring benefits that would not arise if the buyer were a New
  Zealander or the land stayed in current ownership. `[P1]`
- **Farmland must be advertised on the open market to New Zealanders for 30 working days
  before an overseas person may contract.** `[P1]`
- A **Crown pastoral lease transfer separately needs the Commissioner of Crown Lands'
  consent**, and the Commissioner **must consider whether the applicant has unreasonably
  refused public access requests.** `[P1]`
- The Active Investor Plus visa pathway opened in Dec 2025 applies to **high-value
  residential property only. It does not extend to farmland.** `[P2]`

> **The inversion, stated plainly:** the client's end-state — a low-intensity, low-capital,
> access-restricted private ski club — is close to the *least consentable* use of New Zealand
> high country by an overseas person. It scores badly on the benefit test (no jobs, no
> exports, no capital investment) and directly adversely on the Commissioner's public-access
> test. A buyer proposing a *commercial resort with public access* would score far better.
> New Zealand's regime therefore penalises exactly the low-cost discipline that makes this
> thesis attractive. `[E]`

## What New Zealand high country actually costs

Two independent data points converge, and they matter because several asking prices in the
market do not survive contact with them.

| Evidence | Date | Area | Price | $/ha |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lake McKay Station** — completed sale | Jul 2023 | 6,122 ha (5,270 freehold + 852 Crown lease) | **NZ$21.5 m** | **~$3,510/ha** |
| **Cecil Peak Station** — tested, rejected offers | Oct–Dec 2025 | ~13,400 ha | asked $60 m; real offers **$35–45 m**; RV $33 m | **~$2,600–3,400/ha** |

**Extensive New Zealand high country trades at roughly NZ$2,500–4,000/ha** — about a tenth of
the national all-farm median (NZ$28,115/ha, REINZ, Feb 2025) and a quarter of the grazing-farm
median. The discount reflects unimproved land, very low stocking rates, and above all that
much of the area is **leasehold, which carries little transferable value.** `[E]`

At ~NZ$3,000/ha ≈ **RM 7,200/ha**, the *land* is genuinely cheap. The problem is never the
price per hectare; it is that the hectares that are cheap are not the hectares with the
mountain on them.

### Mt Lyford — the only NZ asset that fits, and why it still probably fails

**What it is:** 677 ha of freehold high country in North Canterbury, marketed by Colliers from
Nov 2024, including a lodge, existing subdivided alpine chalet sections and consent for a
further 149 sections. Reported as "some of the highest altitude freehold land in New Zealand"
and "one of the few freehold ski-fields in New Zealand". ~20,000 skier visits in the prior
season. Built from scratch by the Simpson family over 20+ years. `[P2]`

**Why it is interesting:** it passes Gate 1 outright — the rarest thing in New Zealand. The
149-section consent is a built-in capital-recycling mechanism: sell sections to fund mountain
CAPEX, which is precisely how a low-capital private mountain could be financed.

**Three reasons it probably still fails:**

1. **Price.** There is no asking price. The vendor said the value "had to be in the tens of
   millions" — a *vendor aspiration anchored on subdivision value*, not a land valuation.
   149 sections at $300–400k gross is a $45–60 m gross development value before servicing,
   absorption and time. At even $20 m, 677 ha is **~NZ$29,500/ha — roughly 9× the
   $2,600–3,500/ha band that high country actually clears at.** The premium is for a resource
   consent and a going concern, not for land. That is the opposite of buying a cheap rural
   asset (Gate 6, T1). `[E]`
2. **Elevation.** Mount Lyford peaks at ~1,516 m and the ski area tops out below that. North
   Canterbury coastal-range snow at 1,300–1,460 m is marginal now and gets worse. `[E]`
3. **Status unknown.** The EOI campaign ran in November 2024. Whether it sold, was withdrawn
   or remains available was not established. **This is the first call to make.** `[U]`

### Mt Dobson — the honest comparator

At NZ$2.8 m + GST (≈ **RM 6.72 m**) this is by far the cheapest route to control of a genuine
2,000 m New Zealand ski mountain with an existing chairlift — base ~1,600 m, summit ~2,030 m,
~305 m vertical, 400 ha ski area, 18 trails, triple chair + T-bar + platter, and reportedly
the highest ski-field car park in the country. It has sat unsold for roughly 15 months, which
is a negotiating signal. `[P3/P2]`

**But you would own a company and a revocable licence, not title.** It is the direct opposite
of the mandate. It is included here because it forces a question the client should answer
explicitly:

> **Is the real objective to *own* alpine land, or to *control* a private ski mountain?**
> If ownership, Mt Dobson is irrelevant at any price. If control, it is the cheapest such
> asset found anywhere in this study, at roughly 1/20th of Mt Lyford's aspiration.

Three diligence items could not be verified and would decide it: **concession term,
snowmaking capability, and water rights.** `[U]`

## The bottom line on New Zealand terrain

**No New Zealand property was confirmed in which freehold title reaches genuine alpine terrain
above 1,600 m.** `[E]`

Mt Lyford — the only freehold ski field identified — tops out around 1,460–1,516 m. Every
higher ski mountain in the country sits on a concession. If the >1,600 m screen is treated as
absolute, New Zealand may have **no qualifying open-market property at all**, and the
remaining options are to accept a concession over the terrain, accept sub-1,600 m freehold, or
run the unexplored leads below.

### The most promising unexplored ground

**The Inland Kaikōura Range.** It is the one part of New Zealand that plausibly combines
**freehold tenure with genuine alpine elevation** — Tapuae-o-Uenuku reaches 2,885 m, and the
Marlborough stations in that country (Glazebrook 8,877 ha 100 % freehold, Upton Fells 7,857 ha
100 % freehold, Middlehurst, Muzzle, Bluff) came through history with far more freehold than
the Canterbury and Otago runs. This ground was **not reached before the research budget ran
out** and is the highest-value target for the next pass. `[E]`

## Conclusion on New Zealand

New Zealand offers superb alpine terrain, a stable legal system, cheap extensive land and an
established high-country farming economy. It fails this mandate on tenure and screening, not
on quality.

**But there is one clean route, and it is worth stating precisely because it is the only place
in the study where the client's actual thesis — low intensity, private access — can be
consented on its own terms.**

**Section 16 of the Overseas Investment Act 2005** permits consent for non-residential
sensitive land where the applicant **intends to reside in New Zealand indefinitely**. On this
pathway the **benefit test does not apply and public access cannot be imposed as a condition**
— the OIO's own position, as reported in the Mt White Station consent. `[P2]`

The price is emigration: move within 12 months of consent, become ordinarily resident within
two years (permanent residence + 12 months resided + **183 days present** + **New Zealand tax
resident**, meaning worldwide income taxed at up to 39 %), or face a forced sale. The Active
Investor Plus visa supplies the visa limb only — its 21-day presence requirement is
deliberately incompatible with the 183 days needed for tax residence.

So the New Zealand decision reduces to four options, and the client must pick one:

1. **Emigrate.** The s 16 pathway. The only structure that permits the thesis intact. Cost:
   relocation and New Zealand tax residence on worldwide income.
2. **Buy control, not ownership** — a concession-based ski business such as Mt Dobson at
   NZ$2.8 m + GST. Cheap, real, and not ownership.
3. **Buy freehold at a subdivision-driven price** — Mt Lyford, abandoning the cheap-rural-asset
   premise.
4. **Reframe as a commercial tourism development** to satisfy the benefit test — abandoning
   the low-CAPEX, private-access premise, and expecting to concede registered public access.

Options 2–4 each break a different leg of the thesis. Option 1 keeps the thesis whole and
charges for it in a different currency: residence and tax. **That is the New Zealand finding.**

Detail, precedent and the full statutory basis are in `new-zealand-regulatory.md`.
