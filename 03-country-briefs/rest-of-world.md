# Country brief — rest of world

Twenty-two jurisdictions screened on one question: **can a Malaysian buyer hold freehold title
that itself contains skiable alpine terrain and peaks?**

---

## 1. The filter, and why almost everywhere fails it

> **In virtually every mountain country on earth, the zone above treeline is not privately
> ownable.** `[E]`

It is Crown land (Canada), state forest and *mera* common pasture (Türkiye), national park
(Bulgaria, North Macedonia, Montenegro), *monte de utilidad pública* (Spain), *statsallmenning*
(Norway), community pasture committees (Kyrgyzstan), collective land (Morocco), or
constitutionally reserved to citizens (Georgia, Kazakhstan, Armenia, Mongolia, Lesotho,
Greenland — where **no private land ownership exists at all**).

**Two jurisdictions are the exceptions, and both are exceptions by historical accident:**

- **The United States.** Large fee-simple holdings include summits because of three accidents:
  the Homestead Acts; the 19th-century railroad land grants that produced the odd-numbered
  section checkerboard across the mountain West; and above all the **1872 General Mining Law**,
  under which a **patented mining claim conveyed outright fee title** — which is how a
  meaningful number of Colorado and Montana summits are in private hands today. `[E]`
- **Scotland.** Post-feudal abolition (2000/2004) left intact very large, unencumbered private
  sporting estates whose boundaries run over the tops of Munros. `[E]`

## 3. United States — rank 1

### Can a Malaysian buy? Yes — and this is the strongest verified finding in the screen

**Federal:** no prohibition. **AFIDA** imposes **reporting only** — FSA Form 153 within 90 days
of acquiring agricultural land, civil penalty up to 25 % of fair market value for failure to
file. A compliance chore, not a barrier. CFIUS real-estate jurisdiction bites only near listed
military installations and ports — relevant to **site selection**, not nationality. `[P2]`

**State — the 2023–26 wave.** Roughly **36 states** had enacted restrictions by end-2025, but
they **target designated "foreign adversary" countries**. The list as at 1 Jan 2025: **China,
Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Russia, Syria, Venezuela.** Texas SB 17 (effective 1 Sep 2025) names
China, Iran, North Korea and Russia. `[P2]`

> **Malaysia is on none of these lists**, and is not a plausible near-term addition — a
> non-aligned ASEAN state with no adversary designation in any US intelligence or sanctions
> framework. **A Malaysian private buyer is today in the same position as a US buyer in the
> mountain West.** `[E]`

**Two residual risks with a specific shape:**

1. **Texas delegates its list to the DNI Annual Threat Assessment *and to the Governor*.** That
   is a political variable the client cannot control across a 20-year hold. **Mitigation:**
   prefer states whose statute names countries in the statute itself rather than delegating to
   a floating list, and check grandfathering state by state — most statutes are prospective,
   but some impose divestiture. `[E]`
2. A legacy group of states bars **all** alien farmland ownership regardless of nationality —
   Iowa, Minnesota, North Dakota, Oklahoma, and with acreage caps Missouri and Wisconsin.
   **None are mountain states.** Montana, Wyoming, Colorado, Idaho and Utah have no blanket
   bar, and the 2025 Idaho and Utah statutes are adversary-targeted. **Verify each before
   offer.** `[P2/U]`

### Deeded vs USFS permit — the single most important US diligence point

The great majority of US lift-served ski terrain sits on **US Forest Service special-use
permits**, not deeded land. **The base area is fee; the mountain is permit.** `[E]`

1. A headline like *"former ski area, 65 acres, US$5.8 m"* is almost certainly **base-area fee
   land plus a permit**, not a mountain. 65 acres cannot contain a ski mountain.
2. USFS permits are **not freely transferable** — Forest Service consent is required, with
   environmental and financial-capability review. **A buyer can acquire the base and fail to
   get the permit.**
3. A permit is the opposite of this thesis: annual fees on a percentage-of-revenue formula,
   agency oversight, NEPA process for any change, and no ownership of the terrain.

> **The ex-ski-area category is the wrong asset class for this client.** The Eastern candidates
> (Mount Storm NY 88 ac, Mt Jefferson ME 152 ac, Saddleback, Hatley Pointe) have no alpine
> terrain and are far too small for ranch offset. The Western ones sit on USFS permits (Iron
> Mountain CA, Cuchara CO, Antelope Butte WY, Sleeping Giant WY, Cooper CO) — and several are
> community non-profits not for sale, or still operating. **Bluebird Backcountry ran on leased
> terrain and closed in 2023: there is no land asset to buy — strike it from the list.**
> St Mary's Glacier CO at 373 acres is the only one with real land, and it is still an order of
> magnitude too small to carry ranch income. `[E]`

**Redirect to what the thesis actually points at: large deeded mountain ranches of
2,000–20,000+ ha in Montana, Wyoming, Idaho and Colorado, where the alpine comes with the deed
and the cattle, timber and hunting already pay the taxes.**

### Liability — and the finding that reframes the whole staging plan

> **The staging line is the paying-customer line, not the lift line.** `[E]`

| Mode | Regulatory position |
|---|---|
| **(a) Private, non-commercial** — owner, family, invited guests, **no consideration** | No state passenger-tramway licensing, no ANSI B77.1 regime, no statutory ski-area duty set, no commercial GL programme. Covered under a farm/ranch policy plus a personal umbrella. **State recreational-use statutes further limit the landowner's duty to entrants who are not charged a fee — protection that is lost the moment you charge** |
| **(b) Commercial or club** — tickets, memberships, dues, paying guests | State passenger tramway board licensing and ANSI B77.1 for any lift; statutory operator duties (signage, marking, padding, boundary) under the state ski safety acts — Colorado's Ski Safety Act 1979 and the Montana, Utah, Idaho and Wyoming equivalents — which grant strong inherent-risk defences **in exchange for** those duties; commercial GL from a thin specialist market; workers' compensation; OEC-certified patrol; avalanche mitigation programme where terrain warrants |

**US ski general liability is a notoriously hard, thin market and one of the two or three
largest fixed OPEX lines for a small area — precisely why so many small US areas have closed.**
No premium quotation could be obtained in this study and none is guessed. **Treat a broker
quotation as a go/no-go input before any club conversion, not as a detail.** `[U]`

### Property tax — the low-OPEX lever, and its trap

The mountain states tax land in **agricultural or forest classification on productive earning
capacity, not market value.** Colorado assesses agricultural land off a landlord-earnings
capitalisation, producing grazing-land taxes of a very small number of dollars per acre per
year; Montana and Wyoming operate comparable classifications, and **Wyoming has no state income
tax.** `[U — directionally reliable, figures unverified]`

> This is the mechanism that makes a 5,000 ha US mountain ranch cheaper to *hold* than a
> suburban lot, and it is why the US survives the "keep OPEX extremely low" test despite having
> the highest acquisition price in the screen.

**The trap: classification is use-based.** Land that stops being grazed or harvested can be
**reclassified to residential or commercial at market value**, and a commercial ski operation
will reclassify the portion it occupies.

> **Keep the cattle and the timber running. They are not merely income offset — they are the
> tax shield.** This is the sharpest version anywhere in this study of the client's own T5
> proposition.

Also check **water rights** (prior appropriation; severable, and a large part of ranch value)
and **split estate / severed minerals** — a third party may own, and be able to access, the
minerals under your mountain.

## 4. Scotland — rank 2: the cheapest owned peaks, the wrong climate

**No restriction on foreign ownership whatsoever** (the Register of Controlled Interests is a
transparency measure, not a restriction). Estates of 2,000–20,000 ha include **multiple Munros
in title** and are priced on a **capital-value-per-stag** convention, producing very low £/ha
for land containing 1,000 m summits. Income is genuinely diversified: stalking, grouse, salmon,
forestry, plus **Peatland Code / Woodland Carbon Code and wind leasing** — though natural-capital
demand has bid prices up since ~2021. `[U/P2]`

**Two hard defeats:**

1. **Snow.** 600–1,100 m maritime terrain; rain-on-snow at 900 m is normal; repeated failed
   seasons; the Cairngorm funicular has been long out of service.
2. **No exclusivity.** The **Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003** gives statutory public access
   over essentially all land. A private mountain is not legally possible.

**Watch:** the Land Reform (Scotland) Bill **"lotting" regime** (~1,000 ha threshold under
discussion) could force exactly this asset class to be broken up on sale.

## 5. Sweden — rank 3: cheap and legal, but you cannot do what you want with it

**The test is residence, not nationality.** All of Jämtland outside designated *friområden* is
*glesbygd*, requiring a permit for anyone not *folkbokförd* there for a year; **legal persons
always need one**; apply within 3 months or **the acquisition is void**. Sweden repealed
foreign-acquisition control in 1993. **Buy as a natural person — a Malaysian corporate vehicle
is the worst possible structure here.** `[P2/U]`

**Then the defeater: *renskötselrätt*.** Under *rennäringslagen* (1971:437) the Sámi herding
right is a **constitutionally-protected civil property right by *urminnes hävd*** that
**burdens private freehold and is not extinguished by sale.** On *åretruntmarker* — the whole
fjäll west of the *odlingsgränsen* — it confers grazing, migration, camping, wood, fishing and
hunting rights the owner **cannot exclude**. *Girjas* (NJA 2020 s. 3) confirmed its strength.
**Snowcats, snowmobiles and pistes are the archetypal conflicting use** — which is why Swedish
resort expansions take a decade. Add *allemansrätten*, and an exclusive private mountain is
foreclosed across all of Scandinavia. `[P1/P2]`

## 6. Two worth surfacing

- **Spanish Pyrenees (Huesca / Lleida).** No nationality bar, cheap large *fincas* reaching
  2,800 m, real ibex and chamois *caza mayor* income. Caveats: *monte de utilidad pública* on
  the tops, and border-zone military consent for non-EU buyers. **Under-screened — deserves a
  look.**
- **British Columbia cat-ski Commercial Recreation tenures.** The cheapest way on earth to
  *control* huge alpine terrain — just not to own it. Worth understanding as a comparator to
  Mt Dobson: both are "control, not title".

## 7. Verdict

**The United States beats New Zealand, Japan, Chile and Argentina on legal certainty and on the
availability of deeded alpine.** Scotland beats them all on price per owned peak, and fails on
snow. Those are the two to escalate.

**No US listings were verified in this study** — the search budget was exhausted before any
property work could be done. Section 12 of the raw file holds the re-run search plan and broker
