# Country brief — Chile

**Verdict: the strongest ownership regime in the study, on the weakest property evidence.**

Chile is the only jurisdiction examined where a Malaysian non-resident can take **freehold
title to an Andean summit, on the international frontier, with no consent regime, no
residency requirement and no screening test.** That is a rare combination and it directly
satisfies Gates 1 and 2 — the two gates that eliminate most of the world.

The offsetting problem is that this study obtained **no verified Chilean property listings,
prices or snow records** before the research budget was exhausted. The legal case is made;
the commercial case is not yet made. Chile is therefore recorded as the **highest-priority
target for the next research pass**, not as a validated candidate.

---

## 1. Foreign ownership — the headline finding

Two provisions are routinely assumed to block foreign purchase of Chilean border land.
Neither reaches a Malaysian buyer.

| Instrument | Effect | Bites a Malaysian buyer? |
|---|---|---|
| **Ley 20.848** (FDI framework, in force 1 Jan 2016) | Foreign investors governed by the same regime as nationals, without arbitrary discrimination; rights to remit capital and profits | **No** — grants equal treatment `[P1/P2]` |
| **DL 1939/1977, art. 6** | *Fiscal* (State-owned) land within **10 km of the border** or **5 km of the coast** may be acquired only by Chilean persons | **No** — governs the State's disposal of *its own* land, not private conveyancing `[P1 + inference]` |
| **DL 1939/1977, art. 7** | Nationals of **bordering countries** (Argentina, Bolivia, Peru) barred from acquiring real rights in declared *zonas fronterizas* under DFL 4/1967 — on an express reciprocity logic | **No** — nationality-limited. Sources state directly that *"for nationals of other countries this limitation does not exist, even if the property is in a border zone"* `[P1/P2]` |
| **Ley 19.253, art. 13** | *Tierra indígena* inalienable to non-indigenous buyers | **Only if the parcel carries that status** — see §5 |

Note the art. 7 structuring trap: it also catches entities with principal seat in a bordering
country, **or ≥ 40 % of capital held by their nationals, or under their effective control.**
An Argentine co-investor could therefore contaminate an otherwise clean structure.

**Unclosed caveat.** The decrees *declaring* the border zones under DFL 4/1967, and DIFROL's
own guidance, could not be retrieved. The nationality point is solid; the map of declared
zones is not. A Chilean notary will still raise DIFROL questions on a frontier property.

## 2. Buying mechanics

- **RUT (tax ID) is mandatory** but obtainable by a non-resident for the specific purpose of
  buying real estate. No residency, no Chilean company required. `[P2]`
- Process: optional *promesa* → *escritura pública de compraventa* before a *notario* →
  *inscripción* in the Conservador de Bienes Raíces. **Title vests on inscription**,
  typically 1–3 weeks after signature (longer for multi-*rol* rural property). `[P2]`
- **Remote purchase is possible** by *poder notarial especial*, legalised and
  *protocolizado*. Malaysia's Hague Apostille status must be confirmed — consular
  legalisation may be required instead. `[U]`
- **No stamp duty on conveyance** is reported (the *impuesto de timbres y estampillas*
  attaches to credit documents, not the sale). If confirmed, this is a real cost advantage
  over most comparators. `[U — verify with counsel]`

### The genuine Chilean risk: title, not nationality

Chile's Conservador is a **register of inscriptions, not a State-guaranteed Torrens title.**
Rural Andean *fundos* commonly show:

- **títulos no saneados** — chains broken by unregistered inheritances, so the apparent
  seller has no clean title;
- boundaries described by natural features (*"hasta la línea de altas cumbres"*) with no
  modern survey, making the stated *cabida* (hectarage) nominal;
- overlapping *deslindes* with neighbours and with fiscal land above the treeline.

**Assume the stated hectarage is wrong.** A 10-year *estudio de títulos* plus a surveyed
*plano de deslindes*, both as conditions precedent, are non-optional. This is where a cheap
headline price per hectare quietly disappears. `[E]`

## 3. Water — Código de Aguas as reformed by Ley 21.435 (6 April 2022)

Less alarming than the headline "perpetual water rights abolished" suggests:

- New rights are **30-year concessions**, but renewal is **automatic by operation of law
  unless the DGA proves non-use** by reasoned resolution — the burden sits with the
  regulator, not the holder. `[P1]`
- Pre-existing rights must be registered in the **Catastro Público de Aguas**; the deadline
  moved from 2025 to **6 April 2027** (Ley 21.727), with a 2026 bill proposing 2030. `[P1/P2]`
- **Water rights do not automatically pass with the land** — separate real right, separate
  registry. A *fundo* sold "with water" may hold rights that are unregistered, unperfected
  or extinguished. Make DGA verification a condition precedent.

**For this thesis specifically, water matters less than it appears.** Stock water and lodge
domestic supply are small consumptive uses and are compatible with the reformed Code — which
actively favours a holder who *uses* water over one who hoards it. **Snowmaking is the
exposure**: a large seasonal abstraction, assessed against source availability, and in the
drought-affected central Andes probably unobtainable. A natural-snow, low-CAPEX plan is well
aligned with Chilean water law. A plan that later needs snowmaking is not. `[E]`

## 4. The SEIA threshold — the wall between Phase 1 and Phase 2

This is the most consequential planning finding in the study, and it cuts in the client's
favour.

DS 40/2012 art. 3(g.2) expressly lists **"centros o canchas de esquí"** as tourism
development projects requiring environmental assessment, triggering at any one of:

| Trigger | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Built surface | ≥ 5,000 m² |
| Predio surface | ≥ 15,000 m² (1.5 ha) |
| Simultaneous capacity | ≥ 300 persons |

The 1.5 ha predio trigger is trivially exceeded by any real ski area, so **every commercial
ski development enters the SEIA** (a DIA at minimum; a full EIA where native forest,
glaciers, wetlands or indigenous communities are affected).

**But the definition is anchored on *"obras de edificación y urbanización"*.** A private,
non-commercial operation using existing roads, snowcats, snowmobiles, ski touring and
portable tows — with no permanent building or urbanisation works — arguably falls outside
art. 3(g.2) altogether. `[P1 + inference]`

That is precisely the client's Phase 1. **The SEIA is a wall between phases, not a barrier to
entry** — and crossing it is deferred until demand justifies the cost, exactly as the thesis
contemplates.

> **Highest-leverage pre-acquisition step available anywhere in this study:** file a
> *consulta de pertinencia* with the SEA describing the intended Phase 1 operation. It is a
> formal, cheap, published procedure, and it converts the single largest regulatory
> assumption in the Chilean case into a written answer **before** committing capital.

## 5. Forest law and the design rule that follows

Ley 20.283 requires a CONAF-approved *plan de manejo* for all native-forest cutting — but
with **90-day deemed approval on administrative silence** and 15-year validity, which is
workable rather than prohibitive. Penalties for unauthorised cutting are severe (double
commercial value, minimum 5 UTM per affected hectare), and there is a hard **500 m no-cut
buffer around glaciers** — which matters on every Chilean volcano. `[P1]`

**Design rule: buy the ski value above the treeline; hold the forest below it as a separate
managed income asset.** Terrain above the *Nothofagus* line is not *bosque* and Ley 20.283
does not reach it. This maximises regulatory freedom on the mountain while preserving the
forestry leg of the carrying-cost offset. `[E]`

One unverified risk that would materially damage the Araucanía leads: *Araucaria araucana*
is understood to carry Natural Monument protection, which if correct would effectively
foreclose piste cutting through the Araucaria belt of Lonquimay / Malalcahuello / Conguillío.
**Verify before shortlisting that zone.** `[U]`

## 6. Indigenous land — an absolute-nullity risk, parcel by parcel

Ley 19.253 art. 13 makes *tierra indígena* inalienable to non-indigenous buyers, and the
sanction for breach is **nulidad absoluta** — the purchase is void, not merely compensable.
The prohibition attaches to the **status of the parcel, not a geographic zone**, so it does
not blanket-bar purchase in the south; but in Araucanía, Los Ríos and parts of Biobío the
probability that a given *fundo* or a strip within it is affected is materially higher. `[P1]`

**Practical rule: obtain a CONADI certificate of non-indigenous character for every *rol*, as
a condition precedent, anywhere south of the Biobío.**

Separately, the *de facto* land conflict and *tomas* in the Araucanía/Arauco macrozone are a
first-order security risk that this study did not research and cannot characterise. `[U]`

## 7. Holding costs — the highest-value unverified proposition in the study

*Impuesto territorial* on agricultural land is reported at **1.0 % of *avalúo fiscal*** above
an exempt amount, with agricultural reassessment due in 2028. The retrieved exemption figures
were mutually inconsistent and could not be reconciled. `[U — do not rely]`

The question that matters is **how the SII assesses high-altitude, non-arable, non-forested
cordillera terrain.** Chilean agricultural *avalúo* is driven by soil class and productive
capacity; bare rock, scree and permanent snowfield have near-zero productive class. If that
holds, a 5,000–10,000 ha alpine *fundo* may fall largely under the exemption and carry a
trivial annual tax bill — **potentially the single largest holding-cost advantage over New
Zealand and Japan.** `[E]`

This is cheap to settle: *certificados de avalúo fiscal* are public and free from the SII for
any target *rol*. It should be the first check of the next pass.

## 8. Target zones — orientation only, not sourced candidates

No listings were verified. These are reasoned starting points, flagged as such.

1. **Ñuble / Biobío cordillera, ~36 °S** — Nevados de Chillán, Las Trancas, Longaví, Antuco.
   Materially wetter than 33 °S; volcanoes to ~3,000 m with treeline ~1,600–1,800 m giving a
   genuine above-treeline band; land far cheaper than Santiago's cordillera; none of the
   Araucanía security overlay. **Fund this zone first.**
2. **Maule cordillera, ~35.5 °S** — comuna Colbún / sector Curillinque, where an unverified
   7,860 ha *fundo* lead sits. Large parcels plausible; snow less reliable than 36 °S.
3. **Araucanía, ~38 °S** — Lonquimay / Malalcahuello. Best terrain and snow of the three, but
   three compounding risks: possible *Araucaria* protection, best terrain likely inside CONAF
   reserves rather than freehold, and the acute Ley 19.253 and land-conflict overlay.

## 9. Argentina — provisionally out

Deprioritised on two grounds, one verified in outline and one requiring a single check:

- The *Ley de Tierras Rurales* regime was under **active renegotiation in August 2026**, with
  a proposed 25 % foreign cap — the rules are in flux during the acquisition window. `[P2]`
- **Decreto-Ley 15.385/44's frontier security zone** (~150 km, requiring prior State consent
  for foreign acquirers) covers essentially the entire Argentine Andes. If current, this is
  close to disqualifying on its own. **One search closes this question.** `[U]`
