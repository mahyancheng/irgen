# Operating precedents — has this ever actually worked?

The thesis is unusual enough to deserve the question. Four real operations bracket it: two
that prove the low-infrastructure model works, one that proves how it fails, and one that shows
where the money is at the top end.

---

## Silverton Mountain, Colorado — the closest precedent that exists

Founded 1999–2000 by Aaron and Jenny Brill. `[P2]`

| Element | What they actually did |
|---|---|
| **Land** | Assembled **~600 acres of mining claims** — the patented-claim route to deeded alpine |
| **Lift** | One fixed-grip double chair, **recycled from Mammoth Mountain for US$50,000** |
| **Installation** | **Dug the 15 tower foundations by hand.** Brill blasted rock, poured concrete, and worked as assistant surveyor to save money |
| **Base lodge** | A **1,200 sq ft pole tent**, with couches and a keg |
| **Terrain** | 1,819 acres accessed by 1,900 ft (579 m) of vertical |
| **Operating model** | **No grooming, no snowmaking, no intermediate terrain.** Expert-only |
| **Exit** | Sold in 2023 to an Aspen-based adventure travel company |

**Why it matters to this mandate.** It is the working proof of three separate propositions the
client is relying on: that **patented mining claims can be assembled into a skiable freehold
mountain**; that a **second-hand lift and hand-built infrastructure** can open a real ski area
for a trivial fraction of resort cost; and that a mountain with no grooming and no snowmaking
can be a viable business.

> ### ⚠️ But read the warning in it carefully
>
> **The Brills' original plan had no avalanche control and no guiding.** Skiers would take a
> morning briefing and go. **The Bureau of Land Management insisted that blasting and guiding be
> part of the programme**, and Silverton has been a guided-only operation ever since. `[P2]`
>
> **The regulator imposed the two most expensive line items in the business.** This is the
> single strongest argument for the terrain rule in `07-terrain-and-snow/01-framework.md`: if
> you buy avalanche terrain, you may be *required* to run an avalanche programme whether or not
> you planned one. **Buying terrain below 30° is not only a cost choice — it removes the
> regulator's reason to impose one.**

## Mount Bohemia, Michigan — proof that stripping out cost works commercially

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Infrastructure | **No snowmaking, no grooming, no rentals, no terrain park, no ski school, no beginner runs** |
| Snow | Relies entirely on 250–300 inches/yr of lake-effect snow |
| Pricing | **Season pass US$109** (US$147 with fees); day ticket US$95 at any age, any day |
| Position | Single, double and triple black terrain plus three blue runs |

`[P2]` A deliberately niche business that succeeded by **refusing to serve the general market.**
It validates the client's Phase 2 economics: a mountain with almost no fixed plant can sell
access cheaply and still work, provided it is honest about who it is for.

## Bluebird Backcountry, Colorado — the failure, and it is the instructive one

A lift-free, human-powered ski area between Kremmling and Steamboat. **Closed permanently in
July 2023 after four seasons.** `[P2]`

**The demand was not the problem:**

- Sales **up 22 %** and visits **up 36 %** in 2022–23
- All 41 beds sold out every weekend they were available
- Busiest day: 300 visitors

**What killed it:**

- **Ran out of financial runway** with no multi-year capital partner
- **Remote location** — over 60 % of guests were driving three-plus hours each way
- The co-founder's own summary: *"any venture in the ski industry is cash-intensive"*

> ### The difference between Bluebird and this thesis, stated precisely
>
> **Bluebird leased its terrain.** It had **no land asset, no rural income, and no balance
> sheet** — only an operating business burning capital in a remote location.
>
> The client's thesis has all three: **owned land that holds value whether or not anyone skis,
> ranch or forestry income that pays part of the carry, and no obligation to grow.** Bluebird
> failed because it *had* to scale to survive. A private mountain does not.
>
> **That is the strongest argument for the thesis found anywhere in this study — but it only
> holds if the land is genuinely owned and genuinely cheap to hold.** A leased or concession-
> based version of this plan is Bluebird with a better view.

## The private club end — what Phase 2 could become

| Club | Initiation | Annual dues | Condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Yellowstone Club**, Montana | US$400,000–500,000 | ~US$78,000 | Must purchase a property worth **US$2 m+** |
| **Hermitage Club**, Vermont | US$80,000–100,000 (family US$85,000–100,000) | US$4,250–18,500 | — |

`[P2]` Useful mainly as a ceiling. **The Hermitage Club subsequently went into financial
distress** — the private ski club model is not a guaranteed outcome, and it is capital-hungry
in exactly the way Bluebird's founder described.

## Cat-skiing revenue benchmarks

| Operation | Rate |
|---|---|
| **Steamboat Powdercats**, Colorado | **US$11,000** private cat / **US$1,100** per seat, **10-guest maximum** |
| Market general | Private cat ~US$5,000/day; a single seat ~US$575/day |

`[P2]` These confirm the hard ceiling identified in `03-income.md`: **one snowcat carries
10–12 guests a day.** At a US$1,100 seat that is roughly **US$11,000–13,000 of gross revenue
per operating day, per machine** — real money, but bounded by physics, not by marketing.

## What the precedents say, in four lines

1. **The low-infrastructure mountain works.** Silverton and Mount Bohemia both run profitably
   with no snowmaking, no grooming, and second-hand or minimal plant.
2. **The regulator, not the plan, decides whether you run an avalanche programme.** Choose
   terrain that gives them no reason to.
3. **Demand growth does not save a leased ski business.** Bluebird grew 36 % and still closed.
   **Own the land, or do not do it.**
4. **The private club is a possible end state, not a plan.** It is capital-intensive, and at
   least one prominent example failed.
