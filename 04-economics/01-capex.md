## Two findings that should change the plan

### 1. The relocated second-hand T-bar is a trap

Purchase price is only **10–20 % of delivered cost.** Dismantling, a new haul rope,
foundations, helicopter placement, new controls and certification turn a **US$60k** lift into
**US$0.8–2.6 m delivered — 70–100 % of the price of a new one.** `[E]`

> Substituting a T-bar for the rope tow would roughly **triple Tier 3**. **The Phase 1 answer
> is a rope tow, or no lift at all.** This compounds with the regulatory finding that in both
> New Zealand and Japan the step-change is `no lift → any lift`: the lift is expensive *and*
> it triggers a licensing regime. There is no cheap first lift.

### 2. Terrain angle is an economic screen, not just a skiing one

Choosing low-angle, low-consequence terrain avoids **NZ$1.1–4.5 m of Gazex and avalanche-control
capital, NZ$100–330k/yr in running cost, and an entire explosives-licensing regime.** `[E]`

> This is why Gate 4 in the screening framework caps the operating terrain at 30°. It is a
> **cost decision** as much as a safety one, and it should be a field on every property
> screening sheet.

## Phase 3, for scale

One new fixed-grip quad chairlift: **NZ$6–14 m capital plus NZ$100–300k/yr** — *more than the
entire Phase 1 programme at any tier.* `[E]`

That gap is the whole argument for the staged approach. It also sets the bar for Phase 3: a
chairlift must be justified by demand that Phase 1 and 2 have already demonstrated, not by
optimism.
